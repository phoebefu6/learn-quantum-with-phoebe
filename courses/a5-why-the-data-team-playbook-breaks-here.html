Why the data-team playbook breaks here

Every data team runs on five habits it no longer notices. The data is already there to query. You can print any table halfway through a pipeline. A query returns the exact answer. A longer pipeline is slower but still right. And a roadmap plans to a date. A quantum register breaks all five, each for a reason you can draw. Tonight draws them one at a time, with Brindle's own project template on the table, and ends with that template rewritten for a quantum pilot. No machine, vendor or roadmap is named, and no year is given for a useful machine, because the primary sources do not agree on one.

Part 0

Brindle's project template, meet the register

Brindle is a mid-sized retailer, constructed for these sessions, with a 12-million-row customer table, a 4-million-row order table and twenty years of encrypted backups. Its data team has a one-page project template that has shipped every project for a decade: source the data, validate it, iterate on the model, ship on a date. The board would like the vendor's quantum pilot run through the same template. Four sessions have shown what the pilot can and cannot speed up. Tonight asks a different question: if the team ran the pilot the way it runs everything else, which line of the template would fail first, and why.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Five contrasts, each drawn, between a warehouse habit and what happens on a register: the source, the identity of the state you are debugging, the ground truth, the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong. LiveFive habits, five contrasts3 min▶

Each contrast below has the same shape: a habit the data team relies on without thinking, and the specific mechanism on the register that removes it. None of them is a slogan. Each has a figure, and each figure draws where the thing actually breaks.

  • The source. In a warehouse the data is already there. On a register every classical row is written in first, so the source is a cost. Part 1, and session 3 priced it.
  • The identity of the state. In a warehouse you print a dataframe halfway through. On a register a measurement returns one draw and ends the run. Part 1.
  • The ground truth. In a warehouse a query returns the exact answer. On a register every probability is an estimate from shots, and every shot costs a full run. Part 2.
  • The feedback loop. In a warehouse a longer pipeline is slower but right. On a register noise compounds per gate, and depth destroys the answer. Part 2.
  • The cost of being wrong. A warehouse roadmap plans to a date. Here the hardware timeline is contested evidence, and the cost of guessing wrong sits on encrypted data that must stay secret for a long time. Part 3.
Real world

Brindle's constructed project template has four lines and a date box. The head of data engineering filled it in for the pilot in ten minutes: source, the customer table; validate, spot-check intermediate outputs; iterate, add qubits until it works; ship, the vendor's quarter. Every one of those four entries is a habit that works in a warehouse. Tonight walks through why each one fails on a register, and Part 3 rewrites them.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Shot. One full run of a circuit, from preparing the register to measuring it, returning one outcome. Session 1.
  • Mid-circuit measurement. Reading the register before the circuit is finished. It returns one outcome and replaces the state with that outcome, so the rest of the circuit runs on something else.
  • Standard error. The square root of p times one minus p over the number of shots. The same arithmetic as an A/B test, used in session 1.
  • Depth. How many gates the register passes through. Grover on ten qubits at its optimum is 1,060 gates, counting the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each, which undercounts hardware.
  • Depolarising noise. The declared noise model: with probability p per gate the register is scrambled to uniform. One parameter, and kinder than hardware.
  • Secrecy life. How long a piece of data must stay confidential. The one length on tonight's timeline that Brindle can measure.
  • Harvest now, decrypt later. Copying encrypted data today in order to read it once a capable machine exists. Named in the 2023 factsheet and the 2024 NIST draft.
Self-studyWhat tonight does not do2 min▶

Tonight does not teach error correction. It names the fact that fault tolerance is the published long-term answer to noise, and leaves how it works to the literature; nobody on the hub teaches it, and nobody on the hub teaches hardware physics either. The simulator in the page is exact, and the noise layered on top of it is a declared one-parameter model, so every noisy number tonight is modelled and flatters real hardware.

Tonight also does not build the post-quantum inventory. It shows why the inventory matters: the asymmetric cost of being wrong about the timeline sits on long-lived encrypted data. Session 6 turns that into a list, and practitioner session 9 builds it in full.

Part 1 · covers the source and the identity

The data is not there, and you cannot look at it

15 min live

The first two habits are so basic that a data team would not list them as habits at all. The data is there when you start. And you can look at it whenever you like. On a register the first is a bill and the second is impossible, and both for mechanical reasons, not for lack of tooling.

LiveContrast 1: the source is a cost, not a given5 min▶

In a warehouse the first line of a project plan, "source the data", usually means finding the right table and getting permission to read it. The data is already there. Nobody budgets for the table existing.

On a register nothing is already there. Session 3 priced it: every classical row has to be written in before the first query runs, so a load of N rows costs N writes. Under the declared model from that session (load 1 per row, query cost log2 N per oracle call, R = 1 search per load), one search over Brindle's constructed 12,000,000 customer rows costs 12,063,988, of which 12,000,000 is the load. A classical scan costs 6,000,000. The source is not a line you tick; it is the largest line in the budget.

The consequence for the plan is that "source" has to carry two numbers it never needed before: how many rows must be written, and how many searches each load will serve before the data changes. Without the second number the first cannot be judged.

Real world

Brindle's constructed template says "Source: customer table (existing)". The word existing is the habit. On a register the honest entry is "Source: 12,000,000 writes per load, reloaded nightly, so R is however many searches run in a day". The team had never had to write the word nightly into a source line before, because a warehouse never charged them for reading what it already held.

LiveContrast 2: you cannot print the state5 min▶

Every data engineer debugs the same way. Something looks wrong at the end, so you print the table after the join, then after the filter, and find the stage where the row count jumps. It works because reading a dataframe does not change it. You can look as often as you like and the pipeline carries on with exactly what you looked at.

A register does not allow that. Session 1 established that a measurement returns one outcome, drawn with probability equal to the squared amplitude, and that afterwards the amplitudes are gone. So a read halfway through a circuit does not show you the state; it replaces the state with one draw, and the rest of the circuit runs on that draw instead of on what you wanted to inspect. In the figure, the read after the oracle returns 011 and the diffusion step that would have done the work never sees the superposition it needed.

There is no "print" that leaves the state alone. To learn what the register held at a given step you stop the circuit there, measure, and repeat the whole thing from the start many times, building a histogram one run at a time. Each step you want to inspect becomes its own experiment with its own shot budget.

Self-studyWhy the page can show you what hardware cannot4 min▶

Every session so far has shown you the full row of amplitudes: the bars in the figure, the curves in session 2, the table in session 1's widget. That view exists only because the simulator in this page is a classical program. It holds the 2 to the n numbers in ordinary memory and prints them, which is precisely what a real register forbids. The simulator is a teaching instrument and a test harness, and its honesty depends on saying so.

Two consequences for a plan. First, validation done on a simulator, where every amplitude is visible, is not the same activity as validation on hardware, where only counts are. Both are needed, and they are different line items. Second, the simulator only reaches the low twenties of qubits, as session 1's table showed, so beyond that there is no full view to compare against at all, and validation has to lean on problems whose answer is known in advance.

Real world

Brindle's constructed template has a validate line that says "spot-check intermediate outputs". The analytics lead's first rewrite was "spot-check intermediate amplitudes on the vendor's machine". There is no such check. The honest version has two lines: inspect amplitudes on a simulator at small sizes, and on hardware compare sampled outputs against a case whose answer the team already knows.

Part 2 · covers ground truth and the feedback loop

The answer is a sample, and depth makes it wrong

10 min live

The next two habits are about the answer itself. A warehouse query returns an exact number once. A warehouse pipeline that runs longer is slower, never less correct. On a register the answer arrives as counts with an error bar, every count costs a full run, and every extra gate is another chance for the answer to be scrambled.

LiveContrast 3: the ground truth is a sample4 min▶

Leave the widget at 1,000 shots and seed 7. The target comes back 952 times. The honest report is not "the probability is 0.952". It is "an estimate of 0.952 with a standard error of about 0.007", using the square root of p times one minus p over the number of shots, the same arithmetic session 1 used and every A/B test uses. The exact figure, 0.945313, sits inside one standard error of the estimate. You can see it here only because the simulator is a classical program; on hardware it is the thing you are trying to estimate and you never get to read it.

Now drop to 100 shots and change the seed. The estimate moves by several hundredths from draw to draw, and the error bar widens to match. Drop to 10 shots and some draws put every shot on the target, which makes the formula return an error of zero. That is not certainty. It is a sample too small to measure its own uncertainty, and a team that reports it as certain has made the oldest mistake in statistics on the newest machine.

Then look at the shot bill. Each shot is a complete run of the circuit, 31 gates here with the oracle and the multi-controlled flip counted as one each, which undercounts hardware. Halving the error needs four times the shots, so precision is bought in whole runs. In a warehouse the second decimal place is free. Here it has a price, and the plan has to state it.

LiveContrast 4: depth destroys the answer4 min▶

In a warehouse the feedback loop is forgiving. A pipeline that grew from three stages to twelve is slower, maybe embarrassingly so, but the number at the end is still right. So the team's instinct when something is not good enough is to add: more stages, more features, more data.

On a register every gate carries a chance of scrambling the state, and the chances multiply. The noise here is modelled: one global depolarising parameter per gate, which is kinder than hardware because real errors are structured and a real oracle is many gates, not one. Even so, Grover on ten qubits at its ideal depth of 1,060 gates (oracle and multi-controlled flip counted as one gate each, which undercounts hardware) succeeds with probability 0.999461 with no noise. At one error per thousand gates it falls to 0.346724. At one per hundred it is 0.001000, which is the uniform floor of one over 1,024 to three decimals: the circuit is now a very expensive random number generator.

And the instinct to add makes it worse. At p = 0.005 per gate, each qubit count run at its own optimum, success falls from 0.9562 on two qubits to 0.0059 on ten, monotonically, every step of the way. Each added qubit doubles the search space and lengthens the circuit, and length is what the noise charges for. "Add more qubits" is the lever that always makes the number worse.

Real world

Brindle's constructed template says "Iterate: add qubits until it works", which is the warehouse instinct of adding features until the model is good enough. Under realistic noise that loop runs the wrong way. The honest iterate line caps depth first and asks how many gates the answer can survive, and only then asks how big a problem fits under that cap.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: when a useful machine arrives4 min▶

The fifth habit, planning to a date, needs a date to plan to. The primary sources do not supply one, and they disagree in ways that are worth reading side by side rather than averaging.

  • Preskill 2018. Noisy intermediate-scale devices are the near term; noise in the gates limits the size of circuit that can run reliably; more accurate gates and "eventually" fault tolerance are the longer path. No date is given.
  • NIST IR 8547, initial public draft, 2024. Plans as if a capable machine may arrive within the secrecy life of data encrypted today, and says the migration must begin "even if quantum computers are a decade away". It proposes 2030 and 2035 as deprecation and disallowance years for today's public-key algorithms. Those are proposed policy dates in a draft, not a forecast of hardware.
  • Aaronson 2015. Writes "supposing we had a quantum computer" and spends the essay on what it would be good for. The question of when is set aside, not answered.

These sessions resolve nothing and give no year for a useful machine. What they do instead is notice that the three stances lead to the same action on one specific asset. Part 3 draws it.

Sources: Preskill, J. (2018). Quantum computing in the NISQ era and beyond. Quantum 2, 79; arXiv 1801.00862. Moody, D., Perlner, R., Regenscheid, A., Robinson, A. and Cooper, D. (2024). Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards. NIST IR 8547 ipd, initial public draft, November 2024. Aaronson, S. (2015). Quantum machine learning algorithms: read the fine print. Part 3 · build-along

Rewrite the project plan

17 min hands on

No code. Brindle's constructed project template has four lines and a date box: source, validate, iterate, ship on a date. Six steps rewrite it for a quantum pilot, one contrast at a time, and the last line is one the template never had. The drawing is the fifth contrast, the one the date box hides.

Copy the template exactly

Four lines and a box: Source, the customer table (existing). Validate, spot-check intermediate outputs. Iterate, add qubits until it works. Ship, the vendor's quarter. Write them down the left of a page, word for word, and leave the right half empty. Each habit gets rewritten in its own words, not paraphrased away.

Rewrite Source as a cost

Replace "existing" with the load. 12,000,000 writes per load, and how often the table is reloaded, so R, the searches per load, is a number. Copy the session 3 model beside it: load 1 per row, query cost log2 N, R searches per load. If R is 1, write "a scan wins" on the line and stop there; the other lines only matter if the window is open.

Split Validate in two

"Spot-check intermediate outputs" becomes two lines. One: inspect amplitudes on a simulator at small sizes, where the full row is visible because it is a classical program. Two: on hardware, compare sampled outputs against a case whose answer is already known, because there is no intermediate state to print and every look is a fresh experiment.

Add a Measure line with a shot budget

The template never had one, because a warehouse query is exact. Write the error you need, then the shots it costs: near a probability of 0.95, about 0.007 takes 1,000 shots, and halving it takes four times as many. Every shot is a full run of the circuit. Put the number of runs in the budget column, not the number of answers.

Turn Iterate round

Cross out "add qubits until it works" and write "cap depth, then size the problem under the cap". Quote the anti-lever beside it: at a modelled p = 0.005 per gate, success falls from 0.9562 on two qubits to 0.0059 on ten, and the model is kinder than hardware. The question for the vendor is how many gates their answer survives, not how many qubits they have.

Replace the date box with a secrecy life

Delete the vendor's quarter. In its place write the three stances, Preskill "eventually", NIST draft "even if a decade away", Aaronson "supposing we had one", and no year. Under them write the one length Brindle can measure: how long the twenty years of encrypted backups must stay secret. That number, not a vendor date, is what session 6 builds the inventory on.

The habit to keep. Plan on what you can measure: the load in writes, the depth in gates, the precision in shots, and how long your data must stay secret. Never plan on a vendor date. The first four are numbers your team can check this week; the date is a stance in a contested literature, and a plan built on it inherits the dispute.

Check yourself

Three questions

Brindle's analyst wants to debug the pilot circuit the way she debugs a pipeline: print the state after the oracle, then carry on. Why can she not?

A · She can, but printing slows the circuit and adds noise
B · The vendor's tools do not yet support a print command, though a later version might
C · Reading the register returns one sampled outcome and replaces the state with it, so the rest of the circuit runs on that draw; the full row of amplitudes is visible only in a classical simulation, which hardware never offers
This is mechanism, not tooling, so no later version fixes it. A measurement is a draw that ends the superposition the circuit needed. To learn what the register held at a step you stop there, measure, and repeat the whole run many times, which makes each inspection its own experiment with its own shot budget.

Grover on three qubits, target 101, two iterations, 1,000 shots with seed 7, puts 952 shots on the target. What does that tell the team, and with what error?

A · An estimate of 0.952 with a standard error of about 0.007, the square root of p(1 - p) over 1,000; the exact 0.945313 lies within one standard error, and on hardware the exact figure is never available to compare against
B · The target's probability is exactly 0.952, because 1,000 shots is a large sample
C · The circuit is faulty, because 952 is not 945
Counts estimate a probability; they never read it. The gap of about 0.007 between 0.952 and 0.945313 is ordinary sampling, the same arithmetic as an A/B test. Halving the error would take about 4,000 shots, each one a full run of the 31-gate circuit, counted with the oracle as one gate, which undercounts hardware.

A vendor proposes to improve a disappointing result by moving from six qubits to ten at the same error rate per gate. Under the modelled noise, what happens?

A · Success improves, because ten qubits search a larger space with more precision
B · Success falls, because each added qubit doubles the space and lengthens the circuit at its optimum, and noise compounds per gate: at p = 0.005 it goes from 0.4511 on six qubits to 0.0059 on ten
C · Nothing changes, because the noise rate per gate is the same
The rate per gate is the same, but the number of gates is not: 162 at six qubits against 1,060 at ten, counting the oracle as one gate, which undercounts hardware. The kept fraction is (1 - p) to the number of gates, so more gates means less of the answer survives. The model is one global depolarising parameter and kinder than hardware, so the real fall is steeper.
Before session 6

Homework

Twenty minutes, with your own organisation in mind rather than Brindle. Session 6 builds an inventory, and it starts from the list you bring.

List three systems that hold long-lived secrets

Name three systems at your organisation that hold data which must stay secret for more than ten years. Backups and archives are the obvious first place to look; records kept for legal or contractual reasons, and anything personal or medical, are the usual next ones. Session 6 inventories them.

Give each one a secrecy life and an owner

Beside each system write how long its data must stay secret, in years, with the reason, and the name of the team that owns it. Where you do not know the number, write "unknown" and the person who would; that is a finding, not a gap.
