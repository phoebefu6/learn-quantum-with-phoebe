A qubit is a vector, a gate is a matrix

Brindle's board has a vendor deck on the table and two questions for the data team: will this change our workload, and what do we have to do about the encryption. A measurement is a sample, and a sample is something a data team already knows how to handle. Tonight is those three sentences, checked on a simulator that runs in this page rather than described. Part 0

Brindle, on the morning the deck arrives

Brindle is a mid-sized retailer, constructed for these sessions, with a 12-million-row customer table, a 4-million-row order table and twenty years of encrypted backups. A vendor deck has reached the board. It says the word quantum eleven times and the word data four times, and it proposes a pilot. The board has done the right thing and sent it to the data team with two questions. The data team's problem is that every sentence in the deck uses words that sound like words they know. Superposition sounds like parallelism. Qubit sounds like a bigger bit. Speedup sounds like a faster warehouse. None of those readings is right, and the first job is to replace them with three sentences that can be checked.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Three sentences you can say from memory and defend: a qubit is a vector, a gate is a matrix, a measurement is a sample. LiveThree sentences the deck does not contain4 min▶

Ask the room what a qubit is and the answers come back as metaphors: a bit that is zero and one at the same time, a coin spinning in the air, a cat. Metaphors are how the subject is usually sold and they are why the deck cannot be read. Each of them is a picture of a thing that is actually a number.

The honest version is shorter and duller. A qubit's state is two numbers, call them a and b, with a squared plus b squared equal to one. A gate is a two-by-two matrix that multiplies that pair and produces a new pair. A measurement reads the qubit and returns zero with probability a squared or one with probability b squared, and then the pair is gone. That is the whole of it for one qubit, and everything else in the field is what happens when you have more than one.

Real world

Brindle's analytics lead read the deck twice and could not say what any claim would look like if it were false. That is the tell. A claim about a vector, a matrix and a sample can be checked by anyone who can multiply; a claim about a spinning coin cannot be checked by anybody. The deck is written in the second language on purpose, and the data team's first move is to translate it into the first.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Amplitude. One of the numbers in the vector. It can be negative, and that sign is where every speedup comes from. Session 2.
  • Register. Several qubits together. n qubits carry 2 to the power n amplitudes, so ten qubits carry 1,024 of them. Part 2 tonight.
  • Gate. A matrix applied to the register. Hadamard, X, Z and CNOT are the four the practitioner track builds by hand.
  • Measurement, shot. Reading the register returns one basis state, drawn with probability equal to its squared amplitude. One read is a shot; a thousand reads is a sample.
  • Superposition. A vector with more than one non-zero amplitude. It does not mean the machine tries every answer at once; session 2 is the correction.
  • Oracle. The gate that marks the item being searched for by flipping its sign. Removing it is the break button on the bench.
  • Interference. Amplitudes adding when their signs agree and cancelling when they do not. The mechanism, in Grover's own words, is tuning phases so correct paths reinforce and the rest cancel.
  • Noise. Every gate has a chance of scrambling the register. Session 5 and practitioner session 7 price it.
  • Fine print. The conditions a speedup claim depends on, usually about getting the data in and the answer out. Sessions 3 and 4.

Part 1 · covers the vector

A qubit is two numbers whose squares add to one

15 min live

A classical bit is a switch. It is in one of two positions and reading it tells you which. A qubit is a pair of numbers, and reading it does not tell you the numbers. It tells you one of two outcomes, drawn with a probability set by the squares of those numbers. The drawing below is the whole mechanism; every later session is a consequence of it.

LiveAmplitude is not probability4 min▶

The pair (0.707, 0.707) and the pair (0.707, -0.707) give the same probabilities: a half and a half. Read either one a thousand times and the counts are indistinguishable. They are nevertheless different states, and a gate applied to each produces different results. The sign is real; it just cannot be read directly.

This is the single fact that makes the subject more than a noisy coin. Probabilities are never negative, so two probabilities can only add. Amplitudes can be negative, so two amplitudes can cancel. Every quantum speedup that exists is an arrangement in which the wrong answers cancel and the right one adds, and session 2 watches that happen one iteration at a time. For tonight, the thing to hold is narrower: the vector is the state, the squares are the odds, and the squares throw away the sign.

LiveTen qubits is 1,024 numbers, not ten4 min▶

One qubit is a pair. Two qubits are not two pairs; they are four numbers, one for each of the outcomes 00, 01, 10 and 11, with the four squares adding to one. Three qubits are eight numbers. Ten qubits are 1,024 numbers, and that register, all 1,024 amplitudes of it, is what the simulator in this page holds and updates on every gate. It is not a large object. A phone could hold the amplitudes for twenty-five qubits.

The doubling is the honest reason the field exists and the honest reason it is hard. Forty qubits would be about a trillion amplitudes, 2 to the 40th, which is past what a simulator can store, and that is the point at which a real machine would be doing something a classical one cannot imitate step by step. It is also the point at which every one of those amplitudes has to survive every gate without being scrambled, which is session 5.

Keep the two sentences apart. A register of n qubits is described by 2 to the n numbers. It does not compute 2 to the n things; it is 2 to the n things, in the same way a row with ten columns is not ten rows.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: what the vector means4 min▶

Physicists have argued for a century about whether the amplitudes describe something that exists or describe what an observer can predict, and the argument has produced no experiment that settles it. A data leader does not need to hold a position, and should notice when a vendor does: the deck's metaphors usually smuggle one in. "The qubit is in both states at once" is a reading of the vector, not a fact about it, and it is the reading that leads straight to "so it tries every answer at once", which is wrong for reasons session 2 measures.

The position these sessions take is the operational one, which every camp accepts: the vector predicts the sample, the gates update the vector, and anything a claim says beyond that is interpretation. If a sentence in a deck cannot be rewritten as a statement about a vector, a matrix or a sample, treat it as decoration until somebody rewrites it.

LiveRead the box like a data team3 min▶

Leave the angle at 45 degrees, where the two amplitudes are equal, and take 100 shots on Draw A. The split is 43 to 57. The true probability is a half; the estimate from the sample is 0.57. Take 10,000 shots and the estimate tightens to within a couple of hundredths. That is not a quantum fact. It is the standard error of a proportion, the square root of p times one minus p over the number of shots, and at p of one half and a thousand shots it is 0.016. Anyone who has run an A/B test has done this arithmetic.

Now move the angle to 30 degrees. The amplitude for zero becomes 0.866 and its square is 0.75, so three quarters of the shots come back zero. Nothing in the box reveals 0.866 directly. It reveals a count, and the count is consistent with 0.866 and also with 0.860 and 0.872. The register's actual numbers are inferred, never read, and the inference costs shots. Every cost in this subject is eventually paid in shots.

LiveBrindle's deck, translated3 min▶

Three of the deck's claims survive translation into the three sentences, and they become checkable the moment they do. "Quantum search over your customer base" is a claim about a register of about 24 qubits, since 2 to the 24th is about 16 million and Brindle has 12 million rows, and about the number of oracle calls a search needs; session 2 and practitioner session 5 count those calls. "Quantum machine learning on your recommendation data" is a claim about a matrix, and it has a specific published history that session 4 walks through. "Quantum-safe encryption" is not a claim about a quantum machine at all; it is a claim about which algorithms protect Brindle's backups, and session 6 turns it into an inventory.

The claims that do not survive translation are the ones built on the metaphors: "harnesses the power of superposition to evaluate all possibilities simultaneously" names no vector, no matrix and no sample, and the data team's reply should be to ask which of the three it is. That question, asked politely and in writing, is the most useful thing a data leader can do with a quantum pitch, and it is the whole reason tonight exists.

Part 3 · build-along

Translate one page of the deck

17 min hands on

No code. Six steps with the register above and a page of any quantum pitch you have seen, or the Brindle one described in Part 0. The aim is to leave with every claim sorted into vector, matrix, sample or decoration.

Say the three sentences without looking

A qubit is two numbers whose squares add to one. A gate is a matrix that turns them. A measurement is a draw with those squares as the odds. If one of the three will not come, it is the one to practise, and it is usually the third.

Predict before you press

Set the angle to 30 degrees and 1,000 shots. Write down how many zeros you expect before taking the draw. Then press. Then explain the gap between your number and the count to the person next to you in one sentence that uses the word sample.

Size the register Brindle would need

12 million customers need an index, and 2 to the 24th is about 16.8 million, so the register is 24 qubits and holds about 16.8 million amplitudes. Write that down. Then write down what fraction of them would be zero if only Brindle's actual rows were loaded.

Sort the deck's claims

Take each sentence that promises something and mark it V, M, S or D: a claim about a vector, a matrix, a sample, or decoration. A sentence you cannot mark is a D by default. Count the Ds. In most decks they are the majority.

Pick the one claim to test next

Of the V, M and S claims, pick the one that would cost Brindle most if it were true, and write it as a question with a number in it. "How many oracle calls does a search over 12 million rows take" is the right shape. "Is quantum search faster" is not.

Commit to a guess for session 2

A search over 1,024 items. Write down how many lookups a classical search needs for an even chance, and how many oracle calls the quantum version needs. Single numbers, on paper, unhedged. Session 2 measures both on the register in the page.

The habit to keep. When somebody says quantum, ask which of the three they mean: the vector, the matrix or the sample. The claim is almost never wrong about the arithmetic. It is wrong about which of the three it has quietly skipped, and the skipped one is nearly always the sample, because the sample is where the cost lives.

Check yourself

Three questions

Two qubits are prepared as (0.707, 0.707) and (0.707, -0.707). Each is measured a thousand times. What does the data team see, and what does it mean?

A · Different counts, because the second state is mostly one
B · The same counts within sampling error, because the squares are equal; the sign is real but is not visible in a measurement, only in what a later gate does
C · The second qubit cannot be measured because its amplitude is negative
Probabilities are the squares, and 0.707 squared equals negative 0.707 squared. The two states give identical samples and behave differently under gates, which is exactly why amplitudes rather than probabilities are the object of study.

A deck says a ten-qubit register "processes 1,024 values at once". What is the honest reading?

A · Correct: ten qubits run 1,024 computations in parallel
B · Wrong: ten qubits hold ten values
C · The register is described by 1,024 amplitudes, which is a statement about the size of its state, not about computations run; a measurement returns one of the 1,024 outcomes and nothing else
A ten-column row is not ten rows. The 1,024 numbers describe one state, and reading it produces one outcome. Any speedup has to come from arranging those numbers so the wrong outcomes cancel, which is session 2, not from the count of them.

The register reads 43 zeros in 100 shots at an angle of 45 degrees. Brindle's analyst reports "the amplitude for zero is 0.656". What went wrong?

A · A count estimates a probability, and 0.43 is an estimate of a² near 0.5 with a standard error of about 0.05; the amplitude is the square root of the probability and is never read directly, so the honest report is a probability with its uncertainty
B · Nothing: 0.656 is the square root of 0.43
C · The analyst should have reported 0.57, the count for one
The arithmetic in B is right and the inference is wrong. One hundred shots at p of one half have a standard error of 0.05, so 0.43 is consistent with the true half. Reporting a three-decimal amplitude from it is false precision, and the register's numbers are never observed in any case.

Cheat sheet

Sentence one. A qubit is two numbers whose squares add to one. Not a coin, not a cat, not both at once.
Sentence two. A gate is a matrix. Hadamard is four numbers that turn (1, 0) into (0.707, 0.707).
Sentence three. A measurement is a draw with the squares as odds. You get a count, never the numbers.
The sign. (0.707, 0.707) and (0.707, -0.707) sample identically and behave differently. Every speedup lives in that sign.
The register. n qubits, 2 to the n amplitudes. Ten is 1,024. Twenty-four indexes Brindle's customers. Forty is past any simulator.
The sample. Standard error is the square root of p(1 - p) over shots. A thousand shots at a half is 0.016. Costs are paid in shots.
The sort. Every claim is V, M, S or D. Decoration is the default and usually the majority.
The open question. 1,024 items. Classical lookups for an even chance, and quantum oracle calls. Commit to both before session 2.
