From data team to quantum-ready: the transfer map

Five sessions ago Brindle's board asked two questions: will this change our workload, and what do we have to do about the encryption. Tonight answers both, and the answers turn out to be about the data team rather than about the machine. Four skills the team already has are worth more here than in the warehouse. Four things are genuinely new. Four habits that serve the team well every day are actively dangerous on this hardware. And the encryption question, which sounds like a physics problem, is a data inventory with one extra column. Nothing here is investment advice, and no machine, vendor or roadmap is named.

Part 0

Brindle's data team, sorting its own toolkit

Brindle is a mid-sized retailer, constructed for these sessions: 12 million customer rows, 4 million order rows, twenty years of encrypted backups and an unnamed vendor's deck still on the board's table. Session 5 ended with the data team rewriting its own project template for a register it cannot look inside. Tonight the team sorts what it brought into three columns, then picks up the one quantum job it is certain to be handed. That job is not a pilot. It is an inventory, and inventories are what data teams are good at.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A transfer map you can hand to your own team: twelve named skills and habits, each with its method, the class of tool it uses and the session that taught it. A one-page answer to the board's two questions, with numbers you can defend and a disagreement you can state without resolving. The post-quantum inventory as a spec your analysts can start on Monday. And a 90-day reading ladder in which every source is free, and the ones that are official guidance are marked as such.

LiveSix lines in, three columns out3 min▶

The homework from session 5 was the rewritten project plan: six lines, one per habit a data team brings to a new platform. Lay them on the table. Each line is about to land in one of three places. Some habits carry straight over and become more valuable, because the subject is built out of samples and matrices and benchmarks. Some knowledge is missing entirely and has to be brought in from outside the warehouse. And some habits have to be taken out of the toolkit on purpose, because on this hardware they produce confident wrong answers.

The sort matters more than it looks. A team that thinks everything carries over will run a pilot like a warehouse migration and misread every result. A team that thinks nothing carries over will hand the whole subject to a vendor and lose the ability to check the deck. The truth is twelve specific items, four per column, and they are the same twelve the landing page lists.

Real world

Brindle's analytics lead (constructed, like every Brindle detail) brought the six lines and found that two of them, "add capacity when results are slow" and "print intermediate tables to debug", belonged in the column nobody wanted to fill. Both had been good habits for a decade. Both are wrong on a register, for reasons session 5 measured. The lead's comment was the right one: the habits are not bad, they are local.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Transfer map. The three-column sort: carries over, must learn, must unlearn. Part 1.
  • Cryptographically relevant quantum computer. NIST's phrase for a machine able to break today's public-key schemes. No primary source gives it a date.
  • Harvest now, decrypt later. Collecting encrypted data today to decrypt once such a machine exists. The factsheet also calls it "catch now, break later".
  • Secrecy life. How long a dataset must stay secret, in years. The column a data team can fill and a security team usually cannot.
  • Migration time. How long it takes to move a system from a vulnerable scheme to a standardised replacement.
  • Cryptographic inventory. A catalogue of where which cryptography is used, and what it protects. The factsheet's central instruction.
  • ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA. The schemes in FIPS 203, 204 and 205: one key-encapsulation mechanism and two signature schemes.
  • Deprecated, disallowed. NIST's two stages for retiring an algorithm. The dates in NIST IR 8547 are proposed, because the report is a draft.
Self-studyWhat tonight does not do2 min▶

Tonight builds the inventory and stops there. What happens to Brindle's keys afterwards, rotation, custody and access, belongs to the security courses and is named, not taught. Shor's algorithm is the published reason public-key schemes such as RSA and elliptic-curve key exchange are at risk from a large enough machine; it is named here and not run, because a ten-qubit simulator cannot factor anything worth the page. Hardware physics is out of scope across all sixteen sessions.

No year is given for a cryptographically relevant machine, because the primary sources disagree and none of them gives one. The only dated things on this page are standards and proposed policy dates. Where the pilot budget appears, nothing here is investment advice.

Part 1 · covers the transfer map

Four cards cross, four arrive, four are pulled out

15 min live

Draw the data team's toolkit as a column of cards and move them. The skills that carry over slide straight across and come out worth more. The knowledge that is missing does not come from the warehouse at all; it enters from outside, from papers and standards. And the habits that must be unlearned are not left behind by accident. They are pulled out of the column deliberately, because leaving them in is how a competent team produces a wrong answer with a straight face.

LiveCarries over: four skills worth more here5 min▶

These four are not consolation prizes. The subject is made of samples, matrices, benchmarks and inventories, so a team that already does them well starts ahead of a team that knows the physics and none of the data practice.

  • Sampling and standard error. Method: treat every shot count as an estimate of a probability and attach its error, the square root of p times one minus p over the shots; at a half and a thousand shots that is 0.016. Report a probability with its interval, never an amplitude read off a count. Tool class: a statistics notebook or a spreadsheet. Taught in a1 (the 43 / 57 draw), a5 (952 of 1,000 with an error of about 0.007), p1 and p3. Reading: Aaronson 2015, whose fourth caveat is that reading one entry of a quantum output costs about n repetitions.
  • Linear algebra. Method: a gate is a matrix and a register of n qubits is a vector of 2 to the n amplitudes; check the norm after every step, as the simulator's self test does. Tool class: a numerical array library and a statevector simulator. Taught in a1, p1 and p2. Reading: the standard textbook (Nielsen and Chuang, reported), for the gate set only; the matrices are stated from first principles and checked on the simulator.
  • Benchmark discipline. Method: write the expected number before running anything, give the baseline the same assumptions as the challenger, and count queries rather than seconds. Tool class: a seeded bench and an experiment log. Taught in a2 (the committed guesses), a4 (the matched-assumption test), p5 and p10. Reading: Kerenidis and Prakash 2016 against Tang 2018, the cleanest published case of a baseline given the same data access and closing the gap.
  • Data inventory and lineage. Method: catalogue systems, owners and retention, then add two columns, which cryptography protects the data and how long it must stay secret. Tool class: a data catalogue, an asset inventory and a lineage graph. Taught tonight in Part 2 and built in code in practitioner session 9. Reading: the CISA, NSA and NIST factsheet of 2023, whose inventory instructions are a data spec.
Real world

Brindle's data catalogue (constructed) already lists every system that holds customer data, with an owner and a retention period. It took the team one afternoon to see that the post-quantum inventory is that catalogue plus two columns. The security team had been planning to start from a blank sheet. The catalogue was the head start nobody had priced.

LiveMust learn: four things no warehouse holds5 min▶

None of these is hard once it is named. All four are absent from a data team's normal training, and each is the thing a vendor deck relies on the reader not knowing.

  • Amplitudes and interference. Method: follow the amplitude of the target iteration by iteration and expect it to fall past the optimum; on three qubits the second iteration peaks at 0.945 and the third falls to 0.330. Signs that cancel are where every speedup lives, and no sample shows them. Tool class: a statevector simulator. Taught in a1, a2 and p4. Reading: Grover 1996, whose abstract describes the mechanism as phases tuned so correct paths reinforce and the rest cancel.
  • Per-algorithm speedup factors. Method: name the algorithm, then its factor. Unstructured search is a square root: 25 oracle calls against 512 lookups at ten qubits, assuming the oracle is free and the data is already inside the machine. Claimed exponentials elsewhere carry fine print, and one was matched classically. Ask Aaronson's two questions of every claim. Tool class: a claim checklist. Taught in a2, a3, a4, p5 and p8. Reading: Aaronson 2015, Kerenidis and Prakash 2016, Tang 2018.
  • Loading, readout and noise arithmetic. Method: price three costs no deck prints. Loading is order N: on Brindle's 12 million rows one search costs 12,063,988 against 6,000,000 for classical brute force, under a declared model (load 1 per row, query cost log2 N per oracle call, one search per load). Readout costs about n repetitions per entry. Noise compounds per gate: ten qubits at 1,060 gates fall from 0.999461 to 0.346724 at one error per thousand gates, modelled as a global depolarising channel that flatters hardware, with a gate count that undercounts hardware. Tool class: a loading-tax calculator, a noise ladder, a spreadsheet. Taught in a3, a5, p6 and p7. Reading: Giovannetti, Lloyd and Maccone 2008 for the query cost, Aaronson 2015 for the load and readout, Preskill 2018 for noise.
  • The post-quantum standards. Method: know the three FIPS of 13 August 2024, the dates NIST IR 8547 proposes, and what harvest now, decrypt later means for an archive. Tool class: a cryptographic discovery scanner and a certificate inventory. Taught tonight in Part 2 and in p9. Reading: the three FIPS pages, NIST IR 8547 initial public draft, and the factsheet.
LiveMust unlearn: four habits that hurt here5 min▶

Each of these is a good habit in a warehouse, which is exactly why it is dangerous. Nobody questions a habit that has worked for ten years.

  • Debugging by printing state. Why it hurts: a read mid-circuit returns one draw and ends the run, so there is no intermediate table to print. What replaces it: assertions on the final sample, properties checked on a simulator before anything else, and a self test that fails loudly when a gate is wrong. Tool class: a statevector simulator and a property-test suite. Taught in a5 and p1. Reading: the standard textbook (reported) for the measurement postulate; a5 for the operational consequence.
  • More compute is more answer. Why it hurts: every added qubit doubles the space and lengthens the circuit, and at an error rate of 0.005 per gate success falls at every step, from 0.9562 on two qubits to 0.0059 on ten at an error rate of 0.005 per gate, modelled. What replaces it: ask how deep the circuit is before asking how wide. Tool class: a noise ladder. Taught in a5, p7 and p10. Reading: Preskill 2018, whose abstract says noise limits the size of circuit that can run reliably.
  • Reading a speedup as a wall-clock claim. Why it hurts: Grover's figure is N/2 lookups against order square root of N oracle calls, a count of queries that assumes loading and readout cost nothing. What replaces it: translate the claim into calls, then add the load and the readout before comparing. Tool class: a loading-tax calculator. Taught in a2, a3, p5, p6 and p8. Reading: Grover 1996 and Aaronson 2015.
  • Treating the timeline as settled. Why it hurts: a team that waits for a date waits forever, and a team that adopts a vendor's date has adopted a sales figure. What replaces it: plan on the data's secrecy life, which the team can measure, and state the disagreement about the machine without resolving it. Tool class: the inventory itself. Taught in a5 and tonight. Reading: Preskill 2018, NIST IR 8547 draft, Aaronson 2015.

Part 2 · covers the post-quantum inventory

The encryption question is an inventory with one extra column

10 min live

The board asked what Brindle has to do about the encryption. The honest answer does not start with the machine, because nobody can date the machine. It starts with four questions a data team can answer about every system it owns: what is it, which cryptography does it use, how long must its data stay secret, and does that row fail. The drawing is Brindle's constructed estate run through those four questions, with the planning inequality from the NIST draft underneath.

LiveHarvest now, decrypt later, in the sources' own terms3 min▶

The CISA, NSA and NIST factsheet of August 2023 names the threat as "catch now, break later or harvest now, decrypt later": an adversary copies encrypted data today and keeps it until a machine exists that can break the public-key algorithms protecting it. It names RSA, ECDH and ECDSA as the public-key algorithms that will need updating. NIST IR 8547, the November 2024 draft, says the same thing in substance: sensitive data often keeps its value for many years, so the transition is urgent before any such computer exists.

The consequence for a data team is precise. The risk to a dataset does not start on the day a machine arrives. It started on the day the data was first sent or stored under a vulnerable scheme, and it lasts as long as the data must stay secret. That is why the inventory's most important column is not the algorithm. It is the secrecy life, and the secrecy life is a retention question, which data teams answer every week.

Real world

Brindle's archive (constructed) holds twenty years of nightly encrypted backups, and the board's retention policy says each backup must stay secret for twenty years. Put Y = 20 into the inequality and the row needs X + 20 years of safety from today. Under any horizon shorter than that, the row already fails, and none of the three primary sources gives a date that rules a shorter one out. The backups being written tonight are already inside the window.

LiveThe factsheet's inventory, read as a data spec3 min▶

The factsheet tells organisations to set up a project team, run cryptographic discovery and prepare a cryptographic inventory. Read its instructions as a data team would read a requirements document and they become columns and sources:

  • Network protocols. Which protocols use quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography. Source: network and service configuration.
  • End-user systems and servers. Assets on both, including the paths by which software and firmware updates arrive. Source: the asset inventory.
  • CI/CD pipelines. Cryptographic code in the build and release path, where signing usually lives. Source: the pipeline definitions.
  • Sensitive datasets and how long. When and where vulnerable cryptography protects the most sensitive data, with an estimate of how long each dataset needs protecting. Source: the data catalogue and the retention policy.
  • Correlation. Join the result to existing asset, identity and endpoint inventories. Source: whatever the organisation already keeps; this is a join, not a new store.
  • Suppliers. Ask suppliers for lists of the cryptography embedded in their products, because discovery tools may miss it. Brindle's database encryption is that row.

Practitioner session 9 builds this inventory in code, row by row, in The post-quantum inventory. What happens to the keys once the inventory exists, rotation, custody and access, belongs to learn-cyber-security-with-phoebe and is not taught here. The data team's deliverable is the list; the security team's is what it does with it.

LiveWhat NIST standardised, and what it only proposed2 min▶

On 13 August 2024 NIST published three final standards, and encouraged administrators to begin transitioning "as soon as possible":

  • FIPS 203, ML-KEM. The Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard, with parameter sets ML-KEM-512, 768 and 1024. It is how two parties agree a key, the job TLS key exchange does in Brindle's row 1. Its security rests on the Module Learning With Errors problem.
  • FIPS 204, ML-DSA. The Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Standard. A signature scheme, the replacement path for row 2's code signing.
  • FIPS 205, SLH-DSA. The Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Standard, derived from SPHINCS+, which the release describes as a backup signature scheme.

If signatures as a mechanism are new to your team, the clearest walk-through on the hub is in learn-blockchain-with-phoebe.

NIST IR 8547 is different in kind: it is an initial public draft, so its dates are proposals. It proposes that RSA, ECDSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman and the related schemes at 112 bits of security be deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035, and that the same families at 128 bits or more be disallowed after 2035. Those are policy dates for retiring algorithms. They are not a forecast of when a machine will exist, and a board paper that presents them as one has misread the draft.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: the right-hand side4 min▶

The inequality has three terms, and only one of them is contested. X is a project estimate the team can make. Y is a retention figure the business can supply. Z is the time until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, and the three primary sources read for these sessions take three different stances without giving a date.

Preskill 2018 describes noisy devices of 50 to 100 qubits as the near term and fault tolerance as arriving "eventually". The NIST draft plans as if the machine may arrive within the secrecy life of data encrypted today, and says the migration must begin now even if such computers are a decade away. Aaronson 2015 writes "supposing we had a quantum computer", and his essay is about what one would be for, not when. None of the three resolves the others, and this page does not either.

The useful observation is that the inventory does not need Z resolved. A row with Y = 20 fails under every short horizon anyone has argued for, and a row with Y = 1 fails under almost none. The ordering of the rows is the same whichever stance turns out right, which is why the work can start before the argument ends.

Part 3 · build-along

Your 90-day ladder

17 min hands on

No code. Six steps: five rungs of reading and doing over ninety days, then the page you hand the board. Every source on the ladder is free. Each step says whether a source is official guidance or a free primary paper, because a board will ask which is which, and the difference matters: official guidance is what an auditor will hold you to, and a primary paper is what the claim in a deck should be checked against.

Days 1-15: re-run the first three sessions, then read Grover at source

Work through leader sessions 1 to 3 again at your own pace, moving the register by hand, until the three sentences, the square-root count and the loading tax come without notes. Then read Grover 1996 on arXiv (quant-ph/9605043), the abstract first. Free primary paper, not official guidance. Check that its classical figure is N/2 lookups for a one-half chance and that its mechanism sentence is about phases.

Days 16-30: read Aaronson's fine print

Read Aaronson 2015 from the author's own copy of the Nature Physics essay. Free primary paper, not official guidance. List his four caveats in your own words, loading, applying the matrix, conditioning and readout, and write down his two closing questions. Those two questions are the checklist every later claim goes through.

Days 31-60: read the dequantization and the noise paper, then drive the bench

Read Tang 2018 (arXiv 1807.04271) beside Kerenidis and Prakash 2016 (arXiv 1603.08675), and Preskill 2018 (arXiv 1801.00862). All three are free primary papers, not official guidance. Then open the fine-print bench and reproduce three numbers yourself: 25 oracle calls against 512, the one-search loss on 12 million rows with the three loading parameters printed beside it, and the fall to 0.346724 at one error per thousand gates, modelled.

Days 61-75: read the official guidance

Read the FIPS 203, 204 and 205 pages on NIST's site, the CISA, NSA and NIST "Quantum-Readiness" factsheet, and the NIST IR 8547 initial public draft. All five are official. Three of them are final standards; the factsheet is joint guidance; IR 8547 is a draft, so mark its 2030 and 2035 as proposed in every note you take, and check NIST's site for a final version before quoting it.

Days 76-90: draft the first inventory of three systems

Pick three systems you own, one that carries data in transit, one that signs something, and one that stores data for a long time. Fill the four columns from Part 2: system, cryptography used, secrecy life with its reason, flag. Mark every unknown as a finding with the name of the person who would know, and send one supplier the factsheet's question about embedded cryptography. Use the practitioner session 9 template if you want the rows built in code.

Day 90: write the board's one page

Will this change our workload? Not the analytics workload, on any evidence the primary sources support today. The one outright quantum win measured here is a square-root cut in oracle calls for unstructured search, 25 against 512 at ten qubits, and it assumes the data is already inside the machine. On Brindle's 12 million customer rows a single search costs 12,063,988 against 6,000,000 for classical brute force once loading is paid (declared model: load 1 per row, query cost log2 N per oracle call, one search per load), and a classical index wins beyond 4,224 searches per load. The recommender speedup in the deck's family was matched classically in 2018. Recommendation on the pilot budget: hold it until a specific claim survives Aaronson's two questions; nothing here is investment advice.

What do we do about the encryption? Start now, as an inventory, not as a bet on a date. The standards exist (FIPS 203, 204 and 205, August 2024). The retirement dates for RSA and elliptic-curve schemes in NIST IR 8547 are proposed, not final. Our backup archive (constructed, twenty-year secrecy life) already fails the planning inequality under any short horizon, so it is first in the queue. The data team delivers the inventory; the security team owns the keys afterwards.

The habit to keep. Inventory first, timeline argument never. When a meeting turns to when the machine will arrive, bring it back to the two numbers the team can actually collect, migration time and secrecy life, for every system it owns. The argument about the date can run for another decade without changing which row goes first.

Check yourself

Three questions

Brindle's engineers want to keep "debugging by printing state" in their quantum plan, since it served them well in the warehouse. Which column does it belong in, and why?

The simulator can print because it is not hardware, which is exactly the trap: a habit that works on the simulator teaches the wrong instinct for the machine. No tool fixes it, because the constraint is the measurement itself.

The planning inequality says migration time plus secrecy life must not exceed the time until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. What does it need that nobody can supply, and why does the inventory proceed anyway?

2030 and 2035 are proposed policy dates for retiring algorithms, not a forecast of hardware. The secrecy life is a retention question the data team answers routinely. Only the date is missing, and the row ordering does not depend on it.

What does FIPS 203 standardise?

FIPS 203 is ML-KEM, with parameter sets 512, 768 and 1024, resting on the Module Learning With Errors problem. ML-DSA is FIPS 204 and SLH-DSA is FIPS 205; all three were published the same day.

After the course

Homework

An hour, with your own estate in mind rather than Brindle's. The list you brought to session 6 is the starting point.

Draft the inventory of three real systems you own

Four columns: system, cryptography used, how long its data must stay secret with the reason, and the flag. If you would rather build it in code, use the practitioner session 9 template. Mark every unknown as a finding with an owner beside it; an unknown is a result, not a gap.

Order the rows by the inequality, not by the date

Give each row a migration estimate X and a secrecy life Y, and sort by X + Y, longest first. Write one sentence under the table saying that the right-hand side is contested and undated, and naming the three stances. Then hand the top row to whoever owns the keys.

Put the transfer map in front of your team

Read the twelve items aloud and ask each person which red card they still hold. Expect "more compute, more answer" to be the most common. Keep the map; it is the agenda for the next time a deck arrives.
