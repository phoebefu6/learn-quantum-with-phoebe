Part 0

Brindle decides to build the thing it is being sold

A statevector simulator is the least glamorous object in the field and the most useful one for a data team, because it is exact. It holds every amplitude of a small register and applies every gate as the matrix it is, so any claim about a small circuit can be checked to floating-point precision without a machine, a vendor or a queue. The limit is memory: 2 to the n numbers, which is 1,024 at ten qubits and about a trillion at forty. Up to the low twenties it is the ground truth, and it is the ground truth these sessions use.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A working simulator in numpy, about thirty lines, that agrees with the one in this page to the last decimal on the circuits you both run. The index trick that makes a gate on qubit q a loop over pairs of array entries. A seeded sampler that turns amplitudes into the counts a data team receives. And the seven properties the page's engine checks on every load, two of which fail the moment a common fault is reintroduced.

LiveWhat the engine in this page does when it loads4 min▶

Before any widget on any page draws a number, the engine runs seven checks on itself: the register stays normalised through six gates, H on zero gives two equal amplitudes, a Bell pair never reads 01 or 10, two qubits find a marked item with certainty in one Grover iteration, five qubits match the closed form at every iteration, removing the oracle returns the uniform distribution, and noise lowers success without ever going below uniform. If any one fails, the widget says so instead of showing a number.

Two of those checks exist because of faults that are easy to carry without noticing. Build the diffusion step without its X layer and four of the seven fail. Make the oracle a no-op and four fail. A self test that passes on the broken engine is not a test, so both faults were reintroduced on purpose during the build and the failing properties were recorded before any page quoted a number.

Real world

Brindle's analyst had a version of the diffusion step that looked right, ran without error, and reported a success probability of 0.25 on two qubits where the answer is 1.0. Nothing in the code was marked wrong. The only thing that caught it was the comparison against a closed form she had written down before running anything. The habit this session teaches is that one: write the expected number first.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in code terms3 min▶
  • Register. Two float arrays of length N = 2 to the n, real and imaginary parts. Index 0 is every qubit zero.
  • Basis state. One index. Its binary digits are the qubit values; qubit q is bit q of the index, so index 5 on three qubits is 101 and qubit 1 is zero.
  • Amplitude. The complex number at an index. The sum of squared magnitudes over all indices is one, always, after every gate.
  • Single-qubit gate on q. For every index i with bit q clear, pair it with j = i with bit q set, and apply the 2 x 2 matrix to the pair (a_i, a_j). N / 2 pairs, one matrix each.
  • CNOT. For every index with the control bit set and the target bit clear, swap its amplitude with the one at target bit set. A permutation, nothing more.
  • Phase flip. Negate one amplitude. The oracle in session 4 is exactly this, applied to the marked index.
  • Measurement. Compute the squares, build the cumulative sum, draw a uniform number, find the first index whose cumulative exceeds it. Repeat for each shot.
  • Gate count. The engine counts one per gate call. The oracle and the multi-controlled flip each count as one, which undercounts hardware, and the page says so wherever it quotes a depth.
Part 1 · covers the register

The register is an array of 2 to the n complex numbers

12 min live

Three qubits are eight slots. The slot number, written in binary, is the value of each qubit, and the complex number in the slot is the amplitude. That is the entire data structure. The rule that the squared magnitudes sum to one is the invariant, and it is the first thing to assert after every gate.

LiveIndex arithmetic: qubit q is bit q4 min▶

Every operation in the simulator comes down to one question: for a given slot, what is the slot that differs from it only in qubit q? The answer is index XOR (1 shifted left by q), and because XOR with a single bit is its own inverse, each slot has exactly one partner. For a gate on qubit 1 of three qubits, the pairs are (0, 2), (1, 3), (4, 6) and (5, 7). The loop visits each pair once by taking only the slots with bit q clear and computing the partner.

The convention that qubit 0 is the least significant bit is a choice, and the other choice is equally common in textbooks and libraries. It does not matter which you pick as long as you pick once and write it at the top of the file; what does matter is that a Bell pair on qubits 0 and 1 puts its probability on indices 0 and 3 under this convention and on 0 and 3 under the other, which is why the page always labels results by bit string and never by index alone.

LiveTwo float arrays, not one complex object3 min▶

The page's engine keeps the real and imaginary parts in two separate double arrays rather than one array of complex numbers, and the numpy build does the same so the two can be compared element by element. For the circuits in these sessions the imaginary parts stay at zero throughout, because H, X, Z, CNOT and the phase flip are all real matrices. They are kept anyway, because the first gate outside this set, a rotation about the Y or Z axis by an arbitrary angle, would need them, and a simulator that silently drops the imaginary part is one of the faults the self test cannot see.

At ten qubits that is two arrays of 1,024 doubles, 16 kilobytes. At twenty it is 16 megabytes. At thirty it is 16 gigabytes, which is where a laptop stops, and the arithmetic of why is the only honest answer to "how big a machine would you need to simulate".

Self-studyNormalisation is the invariant, and the first assertion3 min▶

Every gate in the set is unitary, which in array terms means the sum of squared magnitudes before equals the sum after, to rounding. The engine's first property runs six gates in a row and checks the sum is one within 1e-12; on this build it comes back as 1.0000000000000004. That last digit is floating point and is the right size for it. A drift to 0.9 or 1.1 would mean a gate was written with the wrong matrix, and it would show up on the first gate, not the hundredth.

The assertion costs one pass over the array, so leave it on. A simulator that normalises after every gate rather than asserting is hiding the bug that the assertion would have caught, in the same way a pipeline that coerces nulls to zero hides the join that produced them.

Self-studyThe sampler is inverse-CDF, which you have written before3 min▶

Drawing an index with probability proportional to its square is the same operation as drawing a category from a weighted table, and the engine does it the plain way: cumulative sum, one uniform number, linear search for the first segment that exceeds it. For 1,024 slots and 10,000 shots that is ten million comparisons, which is nothing. A binary search would be faster and was not worth the lines.

The generator is a 32-bit mixing function seeded by the draw you pick, and the numpy reference used to check the page carries a bit-for-bit port of it so that counts can be compared exactly rather than approximately. Your own build will use numpy's generator, which is a different stream, so your counts will differ from the page's and your probabilities will not. That distinction, exact amplitudes and sampled counts, is the one to keep in mind every time a page quotes a number.

Part 3 · build-along

Thirty lines of numpy

22 min hands on

Open a fresh notebook or file. Each step is a few lines and prints something you can check against the page. Run from a scratch directory; nothing here writes files.

The register

Two float arrays and a rule. Index 0 gets amplitude 1; everything else is 0. Print the norm straight away so you have the invariant in front of you.

Pythonimport numpy as np def new_state(n): N = 1 << n re = np.zeros(N); im = np.zeros(N) re[0] = 1.0 return {"n": n, "N": N, "re": re, "im": im} def probs(s): return s["re"] ** 2 + s["im"] ** 2 s = new_state(3) print("step 1:", s["re"], "norm", float(probs(s).sum())) # step 1: [1. 0. 0. 0. 0. 0. 0. 0.] norm 1.0

Hadamard on one qubit, as a pair loop

Take every index with bit q clear, compute its partner, and apply the 2 x 2 to each pair. Copy the inputs before writing or the second line reads the first line's output.

Pythondef apply_h(s, q): bit = 1 << q i = np.arange(s["N"]); i = i[(i & bit) == 0]; j = i | bit ar, ai = s["re"][i].copy(), s["im"][i].copy() br, bi = s["re"][j].copy(), s["im"][j].copy() r = np.sqrt(0.5) s["re"][i] = (ar + br) * r; s["im"][i] = (ai + bi) * r s["re"][j] = (ar - br) * r; s["im"][j] = (ai - bi) * r apply_h(s, 0) print("step 2:", np.round(s["re"], 4), "norm", round(float(probs(s).sum()), 12)) # step 2: [0.7071 0.7071 0. 0. 0. 0. 0. 0. ] norm 1.0

H on the other two qubits

The same function, twice more. Eight equal amplitudes of 1 over the square root of 8, which is 0.3536. Compare with the register widget above on the same preparation.

Pythonapply_h(s, 1); apply_h(s, 2) print("step 3:", np.round(s["re"], 4), "norm", round(float(probs(s).sum()), 12)) # step 3: [0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536] norm 1.0

X and CNOT are swaps, and a Bell pair is two lines

X swaps each pair. CNOT swaps the pair only where the control bit is set. Then H on qubit 0 followed by CNOT from 0 to 1 gives the pair session 3 is about. Assert the norm.

Pythondef apply_x(s, q): bit = 1 << q i = np.arange(s["N"]); i = i[(i & bit) == 0]; j = i | bit for k in ("re", "im"): a = s[k][i].copy(); s[k][i] = s[k][j]; s[k][j] = a def apply_cnot(s, c, t): cb, tb = 1 << c, 1 << t idx = np.arange(s["N"]) i = idx[((idx & cb) != 0) & ((idx & tb) == 0)]; j = i | tb for k in ("re", "im"): a = s[k][i].copy(); s[k][i] = s[k][j]; s[k][j] = a t = new_state(2); apply_h(t, 0); apply_cnot(t, 0, 1) print("step 4 bell:", np.round(t["re"], 4), "probs", np.round(probs(t), 4)) assert abs(probs(t).sum() - 1) < 1e-12 # step 4 bell: [0.7071 0. 0. 0.7071] probs [0.5 0. 0. 0.5]

The sampler

Squares to probabilities, then a weighted draw. numpy's generator is a different stream from the page's, so your counts will differ from the widget's 115, 106, 94, 106, 103, 113, 77, 86 and your probabilities will not. The two runs below are from one execution of this exact code; yours will match them only if your numpy version draws the same stream.

Pythondef sample(s, shots, seed): p = probs(s) rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) draws = rng.choice(s["N"], size=shots, p=p / p.sum()) return np.bincount(draws, minlength=s["N"]) print("step 5 counts (3 qubits, H on all, 800 shots, seed 11):", sample(s, 800, 11)) print("step 5 counts (bell, 1000 shots, seed 7):", sample(t, 1000, 7)) # step 5 counts (3 qubits, H on all, 800 shots, seed 11): [109 107 107 97 107 93 86 94] # step 5 counts (bell, 1000 shots, seed 7): [502 0 0 498]

Write the expected number before the next gate

Before session 2 you will add Z and a phase flip. Write down now, from the matrices, what H then Z then H does to a certain zero. It should return a certain one. If your build says anything else when you run it, the build is wrong and the prediction is right, and that order of trust is the whole method.

The habit to keep. Predict, then run, then compare, in that order. A simulator that only ever runs is a random-number generator with good branding. The page's engine was built the same way: seven predictions written first, then checked on every load, then checked again against two deliberately broken copies.

Check yourself

Three questions

A gate is applied to qubit 2 of a four-qubit register. How many 2 x 2 matrix products does the simulator perform, and which slots does slot 5 pair with?

A · 16 products; slot 5 pairs with slot 6
B · 4 products; slot 5 pairs with slot 4
C · 8 products, one per pair; slot 5 (0101) pairs with slot 1 (0001), its partner under XOR with 4
Sixteen slots form eight pairs, and the partner of any slot under a gate on qubit q is the slot with bit q flipped. 5 is 0101; flipping bit 2 gives 0001, which is 1. The matrix is applied once per pair, so eight times.

Your build reports a norm of 1.0000000000000004 after six gates. What should you do?

A · Nothing: a deviation in the sixteenth digit is floating point, and the assertion threshold of 1e-12 is there to catch a wrong matrix, which would show up as 0.9 or 1.1 on the first gate
B · Renormalise the register after every gate so the norm reads exactly 1
C · Switch to complex128 arrays, which do not accumulate rounding
The page's own engine returns exactly that value on its first self-test property. Renormalising would hide a genuinely wrong gate behind a clean-looking norm, which is the fault the assertion exists to catch. Complex arrays round identically.

Your numpy build and the page's widget give identical amplitudes for H on three qubits but different counts for 800 shots on the same seed number. What is going on?

A · One of the two simulators has a bug in its gate loop
B · Nothing is wrong: the amplitudes are exact and must agree, while the counts come from two different seeded generators, so they are two valid samples of the same distribution
C · The page's counts are the true values and the numpy counts are an approximation
Amplitudes are deterministic arithmetic and have one right answer. Counts are a sample and have a distribution; two generators with the same seed number produce two different draws from it. Neither sample is truer than the other, and both estimate the same squares.

Homework

About thirty minutes, with your build.

Add Z and a phase flip

Z negates every amplitude whose bit q is set. A phase flip negates one amplitude at a given index. Both are three lines. Then check your prediction from step 6: H, Z, H on a certain zero should give a certain one.

Write a norm assertion into every gate

Have each gate assert the norm within 1e-12 after it runs. Then introduce a fault on purpose, a 0.5 instead of the square root of 0.5 in H, and confirm the assertion fires on the very first call.

Scale it

Build a ten-qubit register and apply H to every qubit. Time it. Then try twenty. Write down the size at which your machine first takes longer than a second, and what the register occupies in memory at that size.

Cheat sheet

The register. Two float arrays of length 2 to the n. Index 0 is all zeros. Qubit q is bit q of the index.
The pair. Partner of i under a gate on q is i XOR (1 shifted by q). Loop over the slots with bit q clear.
H. (a + b) and (a - b), both times the square root of a half. Copy before you write.
X and CNOT. Swaps. CNOT swaps only where the control bit is set. No arithmetic.
The invariant. Sum of squares is 1 within 1e-12 after every gate. Assert it; never renormalise.
The sampler. Cumulative sums, a uniform draw, the first segment that exceeds it. Counts differ by generator; squares do not.
The size. Ten qubits is 16 KB. Twenty is 16 MB. Thirty is 16 GB. That arithmetic is the honest limit of simulation.
The habit. Predict, run, compare. A simulator that only runs is a random-number generator with branding.
