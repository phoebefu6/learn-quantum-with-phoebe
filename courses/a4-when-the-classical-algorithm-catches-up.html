When the classical algorithm catches up

Session 3 priced the step a pitch leaves out: the load. Tonight is the second half of Aaronson's check, the half most decks never run. Suppose the fine print is satisfied and the data sits in exactly the structure the quantum algorithm asked for. Does a classical algorithm, handed the same structure, do the job nearly as fast? For one celebrated quantum recommendation algorithm the published answer is yes. Tonight walks through the 2016 claim, the 2018 classical match, and the test that falls out of them. No machine, vendor or roadmap is named.

Part 0

Exponential against what?

Brindle is a constructed retailer with a 12-million-row customer table, a 4-million-row order table and twenty years of encrypted backups. The second slide of the vendor deck, the summary page the board actually read, promises a "quantum recommendation engine" with an "exponential speedup". A detail page further in calls it a quantum-accelerated recommendation model. The word exponential is a comparison, and a comparison has two sides. The leader's question tonight is short: exponential against WHAT classical algorithm, given the SAME data access?

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. What Kerenidis and Prakash claimed in 2016, in their own complexity expression and with their own condition on it. The memory assumption the claim rests on, and why it is the same assumption session 3 priced. What Tang showed in 2018, in her abstract's terms, and the method that made it work. The difference between "dequantized" and "quantum is over", which is a difference a board will get wrong if nobody says it. And a six-step matched-assumption test you can run on any speedup slide, ending in an honest rewrite of Brindle's headline.

LiveA comparison with one side missing3 min▶

"Exponential speedup" sounds like a property of the quantum algorithm. It is not. It is a property of a pair: the quantum algorithm and some classical algorithm it is being compared against. Change the classical side and the same quantum algorithm can be exponentially faster, polynomially faster, or not faster at all. A slide that names only one side of the pair has not made a claim yet; it has made half of one.

The half that is usually missing has two parts. Which classical algorithm was the baseline, and what was that baseline allowed to read. A data team knows the second part matters more than it sounds. A query against a raw file and the same query against an index are different jobs with different costs, and nobody would compare the index on one side against the raw file on the other and call the gap a speedup.

Real world

Brindle's head of personalisation, in this constructed case, has a recommender in production and knows what it costs to run. She read slide two and wrote one line in the margin: "faster than what we run, or faster than something nobody runs?" That is the question tonight answers for this algorithm, and the habit tonight leaves is asking it of every slide.

Self-studySix words for tonight3 min▶
  • Dequantization. Finding a classical algorithm that matches a quantum one's speed, up to a polynomial, once the classical side is given the same kind of data access. It removes an exponential gap for one algorithm; it says nothing about every algorithm.
  • Low-rank. A matrix that is close to a sum of a few simple patterns. A preference matrix of rank about k says a few tastes explain most of what customers like. The mechanics are taught in learn-recommendation-with-phoebe; tonight only needs the word.
  • l2-norm sampling. Picking a row, or an entry, at random with probability proportional to its squared size. Big entries are picked often, small ones rarely. The classical move at the centre of Part 2.
  • Data structure. The arrangement the data is stored in before any query runs. Here, a tree of squared norms built once from the matrix. The hinge of the whole story.
  • Matched assumptions. Giving both sides of a comparison the same input and the same access to it. A fair speedup is measured under matched assumptions or it is not measured.
  • Polylog. Short for polylogarithmic: a power of the logarithm of the size. Exponentially smaller than the size itself, which is why polylog(mn) against something that reads all mn entries looks like an exponential gap.
Self-studyWhat tonight does not do2 min▶

Tonight does not teach how a recommender works. Matrix factorisation, collaborative filtering and low-rank approximation as a modelling choice belong to learn-recommendation-with-phoebe, linked in Part 1. Here the recommender is the setting for one argument about speedups, nothing more.

Tonight also does not claim that quantum speedups are gone. One algorithm's exponential gap closed to a polynomial under matched assumptions. Grover's square-root advantage in the black-box query model, which session 2 measured, is a proven bound and is untouched. Other proposals that were later given the same treatment are named as an open thread, not surveyed, and no running time is quoted beyond the two papers' own expressions.

Part 1 · covers Kerenidis and Prakash 2016

The 2016 claim, and the tree it stands on

15 min live

In 2016 Iordanis Kerenidis and Anupam Prakash published a quantum algorithm for recommendation systems. It does not rebuild the whole preference matrix. It samples a recommendation from a low-rank approximation of it, and it does so in time that grows only with the logarithm of the matrix size. The drawing is the mechanism, in the order the work happens: one pass to build a structure, then fast draws from that structure.

LiveWhat the paper claimed, in its own terms5 min▶

Take an m by n preference matrix: m customers, n products, an entry wherever a customer has expressed a preference. Assume it is close to rank k, meaning a small number of patterns explain it. The paper's algorithm returns a recommendation for a customer by sampling from a low-rank approximation of the matrix, without ever writing that approximation down. Its claimed running time is O(poly(k) polylog(mn)).

Read the expression in two halves. The polylog(mn) half is the eye-catching one: it grows with the logarithm of the matrix size, so doubling the catalogue barely moves it. The poly(k) half is the one to keep a finger on. The paper says itself that its running time is exponentially smaller than classical time only if the rank k is a small constant. That is the authors' own condition, stated in their own paper, and it belongs on any slide that quotes their result.

Notice what the output is. One sampled recommendation, drawn from a distribution shaped by the low-rank version of the matrix. Not a table of predicted ratings, not the matrix itself. That is session 3's fourth caveat honoured rather than violated: the algorithm asks for one sample because one sample is cheap to read out.

LiveThe memory assumption, stated in the paper5 min▶

The paper is candid about what it assumes. The data sits in a classical data structure to which the algorithm has quantum access, and that structure lets it create the superpositions it needs in polylogarithmic time. Reading an entry costs polylogarithmic time. Building the structure from a plain array costs linear time, one pass over the data. That is the tree in the drawing: each node holds the sum of the squared values beneath it, so a draw can walk from the root to a leaf choosing each branch in proportion to its weight.

The paper also says this is the same memory model as any quantum query algorithm, Grover's included. That sentence is fair and worth taking seriously. Session 2's search assumed an oracle that could be asked about any row in superposition; this algorithm assumes a structure that can be asked about any entry in superposition. Neither assumption is a trick. Both are the price of entry for a query algorithm, and both say nothing about how the data got there.

Real world

In the constructed case, Brindle's preference matrix would be its 12 million customers against its product catalogue, built from the 4 million orders. The deck's slide two shows the per-query cost and no preprocessing step. The detail page, read closely, mentions "a one-time data preparation phase" in a footnote. That footnote is the tree, and the tree is a full pass over Brindle's data before the first recommendation is served.

Self-studyWhy that assumption is where to look4 min▶

Session 3 priced caveat 1, the load: getting N classical rows into a form a quantum algorithm can query costs order N, and no memory design makes that write cheaper. Here the paper has paid that cost openly, as linear-time preprocessing, and amortised it across every query that follows. That is a legitimate move. It is the same move session 3's window made when it shared one load across R searches.

The question it raises is the one Aaronson's check (b) asks. Once a linear-time pass has built a tree of squared norms, the tree is a classical object. It sits in classical memory. A classical algorithm could be handed it too. If the quantum algorithm is fast because the tree makes certain distributions cheap to draw from, the honest next question is whether a classical algorithm can draw from the same distributions just as cheaply. Until 2018 the working belief was that it could not. Part 2 is what happened when somebody checked.

So the place to look on any quantum data slide is the same place every time: the step before the fast step. Session 3 asked what it costs. Tonight asks who else is allowed to use it.

Self-studyWhere the recommender itself is taught1 min▶

How a recommender actually works, what matrix factorisation does, why a low-rank approximation of a preference matrix makes reasonable recommendations, and how to evaluate one, is taught properly in learn-recommendation-with-phoebe, including a session on matrix factorisation. Tonight uses the recommender only as the setting for a speedup argument, and every claim about it here is a claim about running time, not about recommendation quality.

Part 2 · covers Tang 2018 (STOC 2019)

Same tree, classical sampler, polynomial gap

10 min live

In 2018 Ewin Tang posted a classical algorithm for the same recommendation task. It reads from the same kind of data structure the quantum algorithm assumed, and it samples from a rank-k approximation in time that also grows only with the logarithm of the matrix size. The drawing puts the two pipelines side by side, drawing from one tree, and shows what happened to the gap between them.

LiveTang's result, in her abstract's terms4 min▶

The paper is Tang, E. (2019), "A quantum-inspired classical algorithm for recommendation systems", STOC 2019, first posted as arXiv 1807.04271 in July 2018. Its abstract says this. Given the matrix in a data structure that supports l2-norm sampling, which is the classical analogue of the Kerenidis-Prakash memory assumption, a classical algorithm can sample from a rank-k approximation in O(poly(k) log(mn)) time. That is only polynomially slower than the quantum algorithm, and so the quantum algorithm does not give an exponential speedup over classical.

The abstract is also clear about why that mattered. The quantum recommendation algorithm had been previously believed to be one of the strongest candidates for a provable exponential speedup in quantum machine learning. It was not a minor proposal that fell over. It was one of the best examples the field had, and the classical match came from changing one thing about the comparison: what the classical side was allowed to read.

Put the two expressions next to each other. O(poly(k) polylog(mn)) for the quantum algorithm, O(poly(k) log(mn)) for the classical one. Both are logarithmic in the matrix size. Whatever separates them now sits inside the polynomials, and a polynomial gap is a different kind of thing from an exponential one.

LiveThe method: sampling plays the part superposition played3 min▶

Tang's own framing is the one to remember. Manipulating l2-norm sampling distributions plays the role that quantum superpositions play. Look at the drawing again. The quantum pipeline uses the tree to prepare a state whose amplitudes are set by the squared norms, then measures it, and a measurement draws an entry with probability equal to its squared amplitude. The classical pipeline uses the same tree to draw an entry with probability proportional to its squared value directly, walking from the root to a leaf and choosing each branch by weight.

Both end in a draw from a distribution shaped by squared magnitudes. Session 1 taught that a measurement is a sample and that a data team handles samples every day. Tonight is the payoff of that sentence: when the quantum algorithm's useful output is a sample, a classical sampler with the same access can sometimes produce an equivalent sample, and then the question is only how many draws each side needs.

LiveThe matched-assumption lesson3 min▶

Neither algorithm runs on a raw table. The quantum one needs the tree; the classical one needs the tree. Both pay the same linear-time pass to build it. The 2016 exponential gap was measured between a quantum algorithm that had the tree and classical algorithms that, in effect, did not: classical methods that read the whole matrix take time polynomial in mn, and polylog(mn) against that is exponentially smaller. Give the classical side the same tree and the comparison becomes like for like, and like for like it is polynomial.

Aaronson had written the general rule three years earlier, and session 3 put it on the table. Check (a): does the application satisfy all of the fine print? Here, yes, if the tree is built. Check (b): once the fine print is included, does a fast classical algorithm give the same information? Here, also yes. Tang's paper is check (b) carried out in full on one specific, celebrated algorithm. It is the reason the check has two parts.

Real world

Back to Brindle's constructed deck. Its slide two compares a quantum per-query cost, after a preparation step mentioned only in a footnote, against the cost of Brindle's current recommender retraining over the full matrix. That is the 2016 comparison exactly: one side with a prepared structure, the other without. The head of personalisation's margin note was right. Faster than something nobody would run against that structure, and only polynomially apart from a classical sampler that reads the same tree.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Two questions are genuinely open after Tang's result, and tonight teaches the disagreement rather than a verdict.

What is a polynomial gap worth in practice? One reading says the theoretical question was whether the gap was exponential, it is not, and that settles the headline. Another reading says a polynomial gap can still matter: "polynomially slower" says nothing about how large the polynomial is, and if the classical sampler's polynomial in k is of much higher degree than the quantum one's, the quantum algorithm could still win on some realistic sizes once hardware exists. A third reading says practice is decided by constants, data preparation and hardware noise that neither complexity expression contains, so neither side can claim practice from the expressions alone. Neither paper gives running times on real data, and no number is offered here.

Do other quantum machine learning proposals survive the same treatment? Tang's method, give the classical side the same l2-norm sampling access and see what it can do, is a general tool, and nothing about it is specific to recommendation. Which other proposals survive with an exponential gap intact, and under what access assumptions, is an active research question that the primary sources read for these sessions do not settle. The safe position for a leader is not "they all fall" and not "this one was an exception". It is to ask, of each proposal, whether check (b) has been run.

Part 3 · build-along

The matched-assumption test

17 min hands on

No code. Six steps applied to the recommendation slide in Brindle's constructed deck, or to any speedup slide you have in your inbox. The aim is to leave with both sides of the comparison named, the data access matched, and a headline a board can repeat without being wrong.

Name the claimed speedup

Copy the slide's claim word for word: "quantum recommendation engine", "exponential speedup". Then write the quantum side's running time if the slide or its source gives one. For this algorithm it is O(poly(k) polylog(mn)), with the authors' own condition that the gap is exponential only if k is a small constant. If the slide has no expression and no source, write "unstated" and stop here; the next step is a question for the vendor.

Name the classical baseline the claim used

Exponential against what? Write down the classical algorithm on the other side of the comparison. On most slides it is not named. On Brindle's it is implied: the current recommender, retraining over the full matrix. A baseline that reads every entry grows with mn, and polylog(mn) against that is where the word exponential came from.

Ask what data access the quantum algorithm assumed

Find the preparation step. Here it is the tree of squared norms, built in one linear-time pass and queried in polylogarithmic time. Write it down as a line item with its cost: "one full pass over 12 million customers by the catalogue, before the first recommendation". On Brindle's deck it lives in a footnote, which is itself the finding.

Give the classical baseline the same access

Rewrite the baseline as "a classical algorithm that may read the same tree". Then ask whether one is known. For this algorithm the answer is published: Tang's l2-norm sampler, O(poly(k) log(mn)), only polynomially slower. For a slide about some other algorithm, the answer may be "unknown", which is a different thing from "none exists", and should be written as unknown.

Ask Aaronson's two questions

(a) Does the application satisfy all of the fine print: the load, the structure, the conditioning, the readout? (b) With the fine print included, does a fast classical algorithm give the same information? Write yes, no or unknown beside each, with one line of reason. For Brindle's slide: (a) yes, if the tree is built and paid for; (b) yes, Tang 2018.

Rewrite the headline honestly

Something like: "After a one-time pass to build a sampling structure from our data, a quantum algorithm could sample recommendations in time logarithmic in our catalogue; a classical algorithm reading the same structure does the same job only polynomially slower (Tang 2019). The exponential claim compares against a baseline without that structure." Shorten it if you can; do not drop the preparation step or the word polynomially.

The habit to keep. Compare algorithms under matched data access. Never accept a quantum algorithm with a preprocessed structure against a classical one without it. Whatever the quantum side was allowed to read, hand the same thing to the classical side, and only then measure the gap.

Check yourself

Three questions

What did Tang's 2018 paper show about the Kerenidis-Prakash recommendation algorithm?

The quantum algorithm was not wrong; its exponential advantage depended on the comparison. Tang's classical algorithm needs the same kind of preprocessed structure, not the raw table, and her abstract's conclusion is that under matched access the gap is polynomial rather than exponential.

Why is the data structure, the tree of squared norms, the hinge of the whole story?

The paper itself says the structure takes linear time to build from a plain array and sits in classical memory with quantum access. B is false, since Tang's sampler reads it classically. C is exactly what Tang's result refuted: with the same structure, the classical side is only polynomially slower.

A board member hears "the quantum recommendation algorithm was dequantized" and concludes that quantum computers have no speedups left. What is the honest reply?

Dequantization is a statement about one algorithm under one access assumption. In the black-box query model, a classical search needs about N over 2 lookups for an even chance and the quantum one about the square root of N, and that bound is proven, not a belief waiting to be dequantized. C confuses dequantization with simulation; session 1's simulator imitates a circuit, it does not find a faster classical algorithm.

Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, with the six-step test and one slide that is not Brindle's.

Run the test on one other claim

Take the quantum machine learning claim you marked for session 3's homework, or any new one. Run the six steps. Most claims will stall at step 2 because the classical baseline is unnamed; write down that it stalled and where, which is a result in its own right.

List your own team's prepared structures

Name three structures your team builds once so that later queries are cheap: an index, a materialised aggregate, a sample table, an embedding store. Beside each, write what a query against the raw data would cost instead. You are listing the things a fair classical baseline is allowed to use.

Hold one question for session 5

Every running time tonight assumed each step works perfectly and that the answer can be read out cleanly. Write down one thing your data team does to debug a pipeline, such as printing an intermediate table, that you suspect a quantum circuit does not allow.
