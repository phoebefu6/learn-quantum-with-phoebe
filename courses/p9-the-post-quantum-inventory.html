The post-quantum inventory

Eight sessions checked what a quantum machine would do for Brindle's data. Tonight is about what one could do to it. The board's second question, what do we have to do about the encryption, does not need a physicist. It needs a catalogue of which systems use which cryptography, how long the data behind each one must stay secret, and who owns it, scored so the team knows which row to hand on first. You build that catalogue in pandas on twelve constructed systems, score it under two planning horizons, and find out which rows the timeline argument can move and which it cannot.

Part 0

The encryption question arrives as a spreadsheet request

Every other quantum task in these sessions is optional: a team can decline a pilot, ignore a deck, wait out a claim. This one is not. The joint factsheet from CISA, NSA and NIST tells organisations to prepare a cryptographic inventory, and the request lands on whoever already knows where the data lives and how long it is kept. That is the data team. The work is a catalogue, a join and a sort, and the hard column is retention, which the team fills every week for other reasons.

★ What you walk out with today. A declared schema for a cryptographic inventory, eight columns, each with the existing source that fills it. A classifier that sorts algorithm families into at risk, already post-quantum and unknown, with the standard on each replacement path. LiveWhat the factsheet actually asks for4 min▶

The factsheet "Quantum-Readiness: Migration to Post-Quantum Cryptography" (CISA, NSA and NIST, 17 August 2023) tells organisations to set up a project team, run cryptographic discovery and prepare a cryptographic inventory. The inventory covers network protocols, assets on end-user systems and servers including the paths software and firmware updates arrive by, and cryptographic code in build and deployment pipelines. It asks for a record of when and where quantum-vulnerable cryptography protects the most sensitive datasets, with an estimate of how long each dataset needs protecting. It asks for that record to be correlated with existing asset, identity and endpoint inventories, and for suppliers to be asked for their embedded cryptography, because discovery tools may miss it.

Read as a requirements document, that is a table with a primary key, a join to three existing tables, a duration column and an explicit rule for missing values. Leader session 6 drew it as four columns; tonight it gets a declared schema, types, a score and code.

Real world

Brindle (a constructed retailer: 12 million customer rows, 4 million order rows, twenty years of encrypted backups) received the request as an email from the security lead with the factsheet attached and one line: "can you start the list". The data team's first reply was a question back: list of what, keyed on what, and how long is "how long". Tonight is the answer to that reply.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in table terms3 min▶
  • Harvest now, decrypt later. An adversary copies encrypted data today and keeps it until a machine exists that can break the public-key scheme protecting it. The factsheet also calls it "catch now, break later".
  • Algorithm family. The public-key scheme a system relies on: RSA, elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH), finite-field Diffie-Hellman, ECDSA, EdDSA. One column, closed vocabulary.
  • Use. Key exchange (two parties agree a key, so confidentiality is at stake) or signature (a party proves it wrote something, so authenticity is at stake). The two have different threats.
  • Retention years, Y. How long the data must stay secret, or for a signature, how long the signature must be trusted. The column the data team can fill and a scanner cannot.
  • Migration years, X. The owning team's estimate of how long until the system stops using the vulnerable scheme. Data written in that window is still written the old way.
  • Planning horizon, H. An assumed number of years until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. An input to the score, contested, and never a forecast on this page.
  • Exposure. Years of required protection that outlast the horizon, times a sensitivity weight. Zero means clear under that assumption, not safe forever.
  • ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA. The schemes in FIPS 203, 204 and 205, all published 13 August 2024: one key-encapsulation mechanism and two signature schemes.
Self-studyWhere this inventory stops2 min▶

The deliverable is a ranked list: where which cryptography protects what, for how long, owned by whom. What happens to the keys afterwards, rotation, custody and the move to new schemes, belongs to learn-cyber-security-with-phoebe. Who may read the inventory itself, which is a map of the estate's weakest points, is an access-control question for learn-data-access-control-with-phoebe. Both are linked, not retaught.

Shor's algorithm is the published reason RSA and elliptic-curve schemes are at risk from a large enough machine. It is named here and not run: a ten-qubit simulator cannot factor anything worth the page, and nothing in the inventory depends on watching it try.

Part 1 · covers harvest now, decrypt later as a timing problem

Retention, not physics, is the risk variable

10 min live

The risk to a dataset does not begin on the day a machine is switched on. It begins the day the data is sent or stored under a vulnerable scheme, because a copy taken that day can be kept. It ends when the data no longer needs to be secret. So the quantity to measure is how much of a dataset's required secrecy outlasts the time it would take someone to break it, and every term but one is a column the team can fill.

LiveThe planning inequality, as a column calculation4 min▶

NIST IR 8547, the initial public draft of November 2024, states the threat in its own terms: adversaries collect encrypted data now with the goal of decrypting it once quantum technology matures, and because sensitive data often keeps its value for many years, the transition is urgent before any such computer exists. It gives the planning rule in prose: migration time plus required secrecy lifetime must not exceed the time until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists.

In columns that is X + Y against Z. X is per system and comes from the owning team. Y is per dataset and comes from the retention policy. Z is one number for the whole estate, and it is the number nobody has. So the inventory computes X + Y for every row, which needs no argument, and then asks how much of it sits past an assumed horizon H, which is where the argument enters. The overlap is max(0, X + Y - H) years.

Why X belongs in the sum: a system that keeps using a vulnerable scheme for four more years is writing new harvestable copies for four more years, and the last of those copies needs its full Y of secrecy from the day it is written. The bar in the figure starts today and runs to X + Y for exactly that reason.

Real world

Brindle's backup archive (constructed) is written nightly and each backup must stay secret for twenty years. Its owners estimate four years to move the backup keys to a new scheme. X + Y is 24. The analytics export to a marketing agency holds pseudonymised events kept for two years, X + Y of 3. Before anyone argues about H, the inventory has already said the archive's exposure is eight times longer, and no stance on the timeline can reverse that.

LiveThe horizon is contested, so it is an input3 min▶

Three primary sources, three stances, no date. Preskill 2018 describes noisy devices of 50 to 100 qubits as the near term and fault tolerance as arriving "eventually". The NIST IR 8547 draft plans as if the machine may arrive within the secrecy life of data encrypted today, and says the migration must begin now even if quantum computers are a decade away. Aaronson 2015 writes "supposing we had a quantum computer"; his essay is about what one would be for, not when. None resolves the others, and this page does not either.

The practical consequence is a modelling choice: H is a parameter you sweep, never a constant you look up. The build-along runs two values, 4 and 15 years, picked only because they show the mechanism clearly. Neither is a forecast, and a board paper that presents either as one has turned an input into a claim.

The dates that do exist are policy dates. The NIST IR 8547 draft proposes that RSA, ECDSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman and MQV, and RSA key establishment at 112 bits of security be deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035; at 128 bits or more, disallowed after 2035; EdDSA at 128 bits or more, disallowed after 2035. Those are proposed retirement dates for algorithms, in a draft. They say when NIST proposes to stop accepting a scheme, not when a machine will exist.

Self-studySignatures sit in a different lane3 min▶

Harvest now, decrypt later is a confidentiality threat: it needs something secret to have been copied. A signature protects nothing secret. Brindle's till software updates are public; what matters is that a till accepts only updates Brindle signed. The risk there is forgery once a machine exists, for as long as the signature must be trusted, which for firmware in a card terminal can be the life of the terminal.

The inventory keeps both in one table, because the same discovery finds both and the same owners hold both, but the use column separates them and the score reads Y as secrecy life for key exchange and trust life for a signature. The arithmetic is the same; the sentence in the board paper is not. Leader session 6 flagged Brindle's till updates "forgery, not harvest" for this reason. If signatures as a mechanism are new to your team, learn-blockchain-with-phoebe walks through them.

LiveThe schema, and why each column has a named source3 min▶

Eight columns: system (string, the primary key), data_class (a term from the data catalogue), family (closed list), use (key exchange, signature or unknown), retention_years (Y, integer), migration_years (X, integer), sensitivity (integer 1 to 4) and owner (a named team). Each has a source that already exists: the asset inventory, the data catalogue, the discovery scan, the retention policy, the owning team's estimate, the classification policy. The factsheet's instruction to correlate with existing inventories is this: the inventory is a join, not a new store.

The family column is a closed list for the same reason a country column is. Free text produces "RSA", "RSA-2048" and "rsa" as three families, and the classifier in the build-along would silently send two of them to unknown. Declare the list, reject anything outside it on load, and the classifier becomes a dictionary lookup.

Sensitivity uses the scale the team's classification policy already has; Brindle's (constructed) runs 1 to 4, from pseudonymised events to customer identity and payment. For a signature row it reads as how bad a forgery would be. The score multiplies by it, so a scale that has drifted between owners will quietly reorder the list: check it the way you would check a currency column before summing it.

Real world

Brindle's first draft (constructed) had a free-text family column filled by six owners. It held eleven spellings of four families, and the loyalty API was recorded as "DH" with no way to tell finite-field from elliptic-curve. Declaring the list took ten minutes; resolving the loyalty API took one question to its owner, and the answer went in the table with the owner's name beside it.

LiveWhich families the standards replace3 min▶

The factsheet names RSA, ECDH and ECDSA as the public-key algorithms that will need updating. The NIST IR 8547 draft lists the families it proposes to retire: ECDSA, RSA signatures, finite-field Diffie-Hellman and MQV, elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman and MQV, RSA key establishment, and EdDSA. Those are the at-risk values of the family column.

  • Key exchange families go to FIPS 203, ML-KEM. The Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard, published 13 August 2024, with parameter sets ML-KEM-512, 768 and 1024; its security rests on the Module Learning With Errors problem. NIST's release says it is based on CRYSTALS-Kyber.
  • Signature families go to FIPS 204, ML-DSA, or FIPS 205, SLH-DSA. Both published 13 August 2024. ML-DSA is module-lattice-based and based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium; SLH-DSA is stateless and hash-based, derived from SPHINCS+, which the release describes as a backup signature scheme. A fourth, FN-DSA, is planned as FIPS 206.
  • Already post-quantum. A row whose family is ML-KEM, ML-DSA or SLH-DSA scores zero exposure at every horizon. Brindle's staff VPN (constructed) is the one such row.
  • Unknown. Anything the scan could not name. A finding with an owner and a question, never a blank.

NIST's release of 13 August 2024 encourages administrators to begin transitioning "as soon as possible". The release dates are final; the 2030 and 2035 dates in IR 8547 are proposed.

Self-studyThe score, and what the horizon can and cannot reorder4 min▶

Exposure = max(0, X + Y - H) x sensitivity, for every row that is at risk or unknown; zero for a post-quantum row. Ties and zeros are ordered by X + Y, then by name, so the sort is deterministic.

Leader session 6 sorted its four rows by X + Y alone, and correctly said the order does not depend on the timeline. That holds exactly: subtracting the same H from every row cannot reorder them. The moment a second factor enters, the order below the top becomes horizon-dependent. A short-lived, high-sensitivity row (checkout, X + Y of 14, sensitivity 4) beats a long-lived, low-sensitivity row (the supplier contracts archive, X + Y of 22, sensitivity 1) when H is small, because it has plenty of overlap to multiply. As H grows the checkout overlap runs out first and the contracts archive overtakes it. Neither row changed; the assumption did.

That is the finding to carry to the board, and it is more useful than either ranking. Rows whose rank holds across every horizon you can defend (Brindle's backup archive is first from H = 0 until its overlap runs out at 24) can be handed on today without settling anything. Rows whose rank swings are where the disagreement between Preskill, the NIST draft and Aaronson actually costs something, and the paper should say so rather than pick a year to make the swing disappear.

Part 3 · build-along

Twelve rows, one score, two horizons

20 min hands on

One file, five code steps, run in order. The catalogue is constructed: twelve Brindle systems with plausible values, none taken from any real organisation. Nothing is sampled, so every output below is exact, and it was printed by this exact code on pandas 2.1 with Python 3. The widget above holds the same twelve rows and the same rule, so you can check any horizon against it.

Declare the schema, then load the constructed catalogue

The schema is a dictionary of column to type, and the load casts to it. Two assertions run before anything else: the key is unique, and sensitivity is on the declared scale. Twelve rows, eight families including "unknown" and "ML-KEM", eight owners.

Pythonimport pandas as pd SCHEMA = { "system": "str", "data_class": "str", "family": "str", "use": "str", "retention_years": "int", "migration_years": "int", "sensitivity": "int", "owner": "str", } rows = [ # constructed: Brindle's estate, no real organisation ("checkout_tls", "customer identity, payment session", "ECDH", "key exchange", 10, 4, 4, "web platform"), ("till_updates", "till software, integrity", "ECDSA", "signature", 8, 3, 3, "store systems"), ("backup_archive", "full customer and order history", "RSA key establishment", "key exchange", 20, 4, 4, "data platform"), ("customer_db", "customer identity, 12M rows", "unknown", "unknown", 10, 6, 4, "data platform"), ("order_replication", "order history, 4M rows", "ECDH", "key exchange", 7, 2, 2, "data platform"), ("loyalty_api", "loyalty points, contact details", "finite-field DH", "key exchange", 5, 2, 3, "marketing tech"), ("payroll_transfer", "staff pay, tax identifiers", "RSA key establishment", "key exchange", 12, 2, 3, "finance systems"), ("contracts_archive", "supplier contracts", "RSA key establishment", "key exchange", 20, 2, 1, "legal ops"), ("staff_vpn", "internal traffic, mixed", "ML-KEM", "key exchange", 3, 0, 2, "IT operations"), ("invoice_signing", "signed invoices", "RSA signature", "signature", 10, 2, 2, "finance systems"), ("analytics_export", "pseudonymised purchase events", "ECDH", "key exchange", 2, 1, 1, "analytics"), ("terminal_firmware", "card terminal firmware, integrity", "EdDSA", "signature", 12, 5, 3, "store systems"), ] cat = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=list(SCHEMA)).astype(SCHEMA) assert cat["system"].is_unique and cat["sensitivity"].between(1, 4).all() print(cat.shape, "| families:", cat["family"].nunique(), "| owners:", cat["owner"].nunique()) # (12, 8) | families: 8 | owners: 8

Classify each family and name its replacement path

The at-risk list is the families the factsheet and the NIST IR 8547 draft name; each maps to the standard on its replacement path. Anything already post-quantum is marked so. Everything else is unknown, and the status string carries the action, so nobody reads a blank as safe.

PythonAT_RISK = { # family -> standard on the replacement path "RSA key establishment": "FIPS 203 ML-KEM", "ECDH": "FIPS 203 ML-KEM", "finite-field DH": "FIPS 203 ML-KEM", "RSA signature": "FIPS 204 ML-DSA or FIPS 205 SLH-DSA", "ECDSA": "FIPS 204 ML-DSA or FIPS 205 SLH-DSA", "EdDSA": "FIPS 204 ML-DSA or FIPS 205 SLH-DSA", } ALREADY = {"ML-KEM", "ML-DSA", "SLH-DSA"} def classify(f): if f in AT_RISK: return "at risk" if f in ALREADY: return "post-quantum" return "unknown: ask the supplier" cat["status"] = cat["family"].map(classify) cat["replace_with"] = cat["family"].map(AT_RISK).fillna("-") print(cat.groupby(["status", "replace_with"]).size().to_string()) # status replace_with # at risk FIPS 203 ML-KEM 7 # FIPS 204 ML-DSA or FIPS 205 SLH-DSA 3 # post-quantum - 1 # unknown: ask the supplier - 1

Score exposure under a horizon

X + Y is the bar from Part 1; the overlap past H is clipped at zero; sensitivity weights it. The unknown row counts as at risk, because "not post-quantum" is the only status that zeroes a row. The sort is deterministic: exposure, then X + Y, then name.

Pythondef score(cat, horizon): out = cat.copy() out["xy"] = out["migration_years"] + out["retention_years"] overhang = (out["xy"] - horizon).clip(lower=0) live = out["status"] != "post-quantum" # unknown counts as at risk out["exposure"] = overhang * out["sensitivity"] * live out = out.sort_values(["exposure", "xy", "system"], ascending=[False, False, True]) out["rank"] = range(1, len(out) + 1) return out.set_index("system") short, long_ = score(cat, 4), score(cat, 15) print(short[["xy", "sensitivity", "exposure", "rank"]].head(6).to_string()) # xy sensitivity exposure rank # system # backup_archive 24 4 80 1 # customer_db 16 4 48 2 # checkout_tls 14 4 40 3 # terminal_firmware 17 3 39 4 # payroll_transfer 14 3 30 5 # till_updates 11 3 21 6

Rank under both horizons and name the moves

Join the two rankings on the key and subtract. A positive move is a climb. This is the table in the figure above, and the one to paste under the board paper's recommendation.

Pythonboth = short[["rank", "exposure"]].join(long_[["rank", "exposure"]], lsuffix="_h4", rsuffix="_h15") both["move"] = both["rank_h4"] - both["rank_h15"] # positive = moved up print(both.sort_values("rank_h15").to_string()) # rank_h4 exposure_h4 rank_h15 exposure_h15 move # system # backup_archive 1 80 1 36 0 # contracts_archive 7 18 2 7 5 # terminal_firmware 4 39 3 6 1 # customer_db 2 48 4 4 -2 # checkout_tls 3 40 5 0 -2 # payroll_transfer 5 30 6 0 -1 # invoice_signing 8 16 7 0 1 # till_updates 6 21 8 0 -2 # order_replication 9 10 9 0 0 # loyalty_api 10 9 10 0 0 # analytics_export 11 0 11 0 0 # staff_vpn 12 0 12 0 0

Assert what the horizon cannot do, and check the two edge rows

Sorting by X + Y alone gives the same order at both horizons, which is leader session 6's claim, now asserted. Then list the rows whose rank changed, and confirm the unknown row scored as the worst case and the post-quantum row as zero. Three of the seven moves (payroll_transfer, invoice_signing, till_updates) happen only because those rows cleared and fell into X + Y order; say so when you report them.

Pythonxy_short = list(short.sort_values(["xy", "system"], ascending=[False, True]).index) xy_long = list(long_.sort_values(["xy", "system"], ascending=[False, True]).index) assert xy_short == xy_long # session a6's X + Y order: no horizon moves it moved = both.index[both["move"] != 0].tolist() print("rank changed between the two horizons:", moved) print("unknown row, h=4:", short.loc["customer_db", "exposure"], "| post-quantum row:", short.loc["staff_vpn", "exposure"]) # rank changed between the two horizons: ['customer_db', 'checkout_tls', 'terminal_firmware', 'payroll_transfer', 'till_updates', 'contracts_archive', 'invoice_signing'] # unknown row, h=4: 48 | post-quantum row: 0

Write the paragraph before the next horizon

Before you sweep H from 0 to 30 in the homework, write down which rows you expect to swing. Rows with a large X + Y and a low sensitivity rise as H grows; rows with a short X + Y and a high sensitivity fall and then clear. If the sweep surprises you, find the row that did it and read its four numbers before trusting the sort.

The habit to keep. Never publish a ranking computed at one horizon. Run at least two, report the rows that hold and the rows that move, and let the contested input stay visibly contested. The rows that hold go to the key owners on Monday; the rows that move go into the paper as the part of the decision the timeline argument actually controls.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why is the retention column, rather than the algorithm column, the one that orders a post-quantum inventory?

A · Because the algorithm column is always the same across an estate
B · Because under harvest now, decrypt later the risk starts when vulnerable data is sent or stored and lasts as long as it must stay secret, so years of required secrecy past the horizon are the exposure
C · Because retention determines which FIPS standard replaces the algorithm
The family says whether a row is at risk at all; how long the data must stay secret, plus how long the old scheme stays in use, says how much of its life outlasts any horizon. The standard on the replacement path follows from the use, key exchange or signature, not from retention.

The customer database's encryption is embedded by a supplier and discovery cannot name the family. How should the row be scored?

A · As at risk, the worst case, with the status recording a finding and the question to the supplier; scoring it as zero would rank the least-understood row as the safest
B · As zero until the supplier replies, since unknown data should not distort the ranking
C · Leave it out of the inventory until the family is known
A blank that scores as best is the classic catalogue defect: in the widget, switching the rule to zero drops customer_db from second toward the bottom. The factsheet tells organisations to ask suppliers for embedded cryptography precisely because discovery tools may miss it; the row stays in, flagged, with an owner.

Your ranking at H = 4 puts checkout third; at H = 15 checkout clears and the contracts archive is second. What belongs in the board paper?

A · The H = 15 ranking, because the NIST IR 8547 draft gives 2035 as the year
B · Whichever ranking matches the most recent vendor estimate of the timeline
C · Both: the backup archive first under every horizon tested, the rows that move named, and a sentence that the horizon is contested (Preskill "eventually", the NIST draft "even if a decade away", Aaronson "supposing we had one") and was not resolved
2030 and 2035 are proposed policy dates for retiring algorithms, not a forecast of a machine, and no primary source gives a year for one. A ranking that holds across horizons can be acted on now; the rows that swing are where the disagreement costs something, and the paper should show that rather than hide it behind one chosen year.
