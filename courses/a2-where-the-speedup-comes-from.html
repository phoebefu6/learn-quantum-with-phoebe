Where the speedup actually comes from

The deck on Brindle's table says the machine "evaluates all possibilities at once". Grover's own 1996 abstract, the paper every quantum search claim descends from, says something narrower and checkable: the operations are tuned so that the correct path reinforces and the rest cancel. Those are not two ways of saying the same thing. One is a picture, the other is a mechanism with a number attached. Tonight watches one amplitude climb, iteration by iteration, on the register in this page, and then checks the two numbers everyone wrote down at the end of session 1: classical lookups for an even chance over 1,024 items, which is 512, against quantum oracle calls, which is 25. Brindle is constructed, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Brindle, and the one sentence in the deck that sounds like a mechanism

Brindle is a mid-sized retailer, constructed for these sessions, with a 12-million-row customer table, a 4-million-row order table and twenty years of encrypted backups. The vendor deck the board forwarded has one sentence that the data team keeps returning to, because it is the only sentence that sounds like it explains anything: "harnesses superposition to evaluate all possibilities simultaneously". Session 1 sorted it as decoration, since it names no vector, no matrix and no sample. Tonight is the harder job, which is to say what the real mechanism is in words a board can repeat, and then to show it happening, number by number, on a register the page actually holds.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. The mechanism in one sentence you can defend: one vector, one sign flip, one reflection, one read, and the wrong answers cancel. The two numbers from session 1 checked against the register, not against a slide: 512 classical lookups for an even chance over 1,024 items, against 25 quantum oracle calls, a factor of about 20. And the shape of that factor, which is a square root and not an exponential, so that when a deck says "exponential" about a search you know which word to circle.

LiveThe sentence in the deck and the sentence in the paper3 min▶

The deck's sentence is about evaluating everything at once. The paper's sentence, in substance, is this. Any classical method for finding one entry in an unsorted directory of N entries, whether it checks them in order or at random, needs at least N over 2 lookups to find the target with a one-half chance. The quantum algorithm needs on the order of the square root of N steps. And the way it does that is not by looking at every entry: it tunes the phases, which session 1 called the signs, so that the correct path reinforces and the others cancel. Grover's abstract also claims the method is within a small constant factor of the fastest possible quantum search, which is a sentence the end of Part 2 returns to.

Three things in that paragraph are checkable tonight. N over 2 is arithmetic. The square root of N is a count the register can produce. And "reinforce and cancel" is something the register can show, one amplitude at a time, if the drawing is honest about signs. The deck's sentence offers nothing to check, which is the difference between a mechanism and a picture.

Real world

Brindle's analytics lead, in the constructed version of this story, put the two sentences side by side in an email to the board and asked a single question: if the machine evaluates all possibilities at once, why does the paper that invented the method say it needs about the square root of N steps rather than one? The vendor's reply took four days and did not answer it. That gap is the most useful thing the data team learned from the pilot proposal, and it cost nothing.

Self-studySix words for tonight3 min▶
  • Amplitude. One of the 2 to the n numbers in the register. It carries a sign. Its square is the probability of reading that slot. Session 1.
  • Interference. What happens when amplitudes are added: same sign and they grow, opposite sign and they cancel. The whole mechanism lives here.
  • Oracle. The gate that flips the sign of the one slot being searched for. It is the question the team asks the register, encoded as a gate, and it is counted as one gate tonight.
  • Diffusion. The gate that reflects every amplitude about their mean. It is the move that turns one flipped sign into a taller bar.
  • Iteration. One oracle flip followed by one diffusion. The amplitude of the marked slot climbs a fixed step per iteration, then keeps turning past the top.
  • Optimum. The iteration at which the marked slot's probability peaks. About pi over 4 times the square root of N, read off the curve rather than quoted; the floor of that, or one below, at every size from 2 to 10 qubits.
Part 1 · covers interference, not parallelism

Not every answer at once

15 min live

The picture the deck sells is a crowd of workers, 2 to the n of them, each computing its own answer, with the machine then reading them all. The mechanism the paper describes is one vector of 2 to the n amplitudes, two moves applied to that vector, and one read that returns one outcome. The drawing below puts the two side by side with the signs visible, because the signs are the entire difference.

LiveWhy a sample of a uniform register is useless4 min▶

Start where every search starts: a Hadamard on each qubit, which turns the certain zero of session 1 into a register in which every slot has the same amplitude. On three qubits that is eight amplitudes of 0.354 each, and 0.354 squared is 0.125. On ten qubits it is 1,024 amplitudes, each with a square of 0.000977. The deck would call this state "all possibilities at once", and in the narrow sense that every slot has a non-zero amplitude, it is.

Now read it. A measurement returns one slot, drawn with the squares as odds, and every square is the same. So the read returns a random slot, and the chance it is the one being searched for is 1 over N: 0.125 on three qubits, 0.000977 on ten, straight from the canon the page computes. That is exactly the odds of guessing, and guessing did not need a quantum register. A uniform superposition, on its own, is worth nothing to a search. Every bit of value has to be added afterwards, by making the signs do something, and that is what the next card is about.

Real world

Brindle's analyst, in the constructed story, tried the deck's sentence against this fact in one line: "if the register already holds every possibility, why does a read return a one-in-1,024 guess?" She then read Grover's abstract and found the answer in its own words, which is that the work is in the phases. The deck had described the starting line and called it the finish.

LiveThe two moves: flip one sign, reflect about the mean5 min▶

Move one is the oracle. It is a gate that knows the question and answers it the only way a gate can: it flips the sign of the amplitude on the one slot that matches, and leaves every other amplitude alone. Nothing about the probabilities has changed yet, since a square throws the sign away. If you read the register now you would still get a 1 in N guess. What has changed is invisible to a read and visible to the next gate.

Move two is the diffusion, and it does not know the answer. It takes the mean of all the amplitudes and reflects every one of them about that mean: each amplitude becomes twice the mean minus itself. Because one amplitude is now negative, the mean has dropped slightly below where the others sit, so each of them lands a little lower than it was. The flipped one, being far below the mean, lands far above it. On three qubits the marked slot goes from 0.354 to 0.884 in that single reflection, and 0.884 squared is the 0.781 in the canon table. The other seven shrink to 0.177 each, and their squares still add up with the marked one to exactly one, because nothing was created, only moved.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth saying in the board's language. The oracle marks one answer with a sign nobody can see. The diffusion converts that sign into height, taking a little from every wrong answer and giving it to the right one. The wrong answers cancel each other down; the right one reinforces. Do it again and the marked slot climbs again. There is no step at which all the answers are evaluated, and no step at which more than one is read.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: the word parallelism4 min▶

Physicists do use the word. Some describe the amplitudes as evolving in parallel, every one of the 2 to the n of them updated by every gate, and the description is faithful to the arithmetic: the simulator in this page does update all 1,024 amplitudes on every gate. Others refuse the word on the grounds that it invites exactly the reading the deck sells, that the machine computes 2 to the n answers and hands them all over. The dispute is partly about language and partly about what the vector is, which session 1 flagged as a century-old argument with no settling experiment.

Nothing tonight resolves it, and a data leader does not have to. The operational fact that every camp accepts is narrower: a measurement returns one outcome, drawn with the squares as odds, and the number of oracle calls a search needs is about the square root of N, not one. Whichever word a physicist prefers, the count is the same, and the count is what the board is being asked to pay for. If a deck says "parallel", ask for the count of oracle calls; if it gives one, the word was harmless; if it cannot, the word was doing the work the number should have done.

LiveRead the box4 min▶

Pick two qubits first, because it is the one size where the answer is clean: after a single iteration the marked slot reads 1.000000, certainty, and the second iteration takes it straight back down to 0.25. Then three qubits, the figure above: 0.945 at iteration two, then 0.330 at iteration three. The amplitude is not filling a bucket; it is rotating, and the diffusion keeps turning it whether or not it has passed the top. The engine's self test checks every point on five qubits against the closed form sin squared of (2k + 1) theta, and the worst gap is in the fifteenth decimal place.

Now ten qubits, which is the size session 1 asked you to guess for. The optimum is 25 iterations, and the marked slot reads 0.999461 there. A classical search over 1,024 items needs 512 lookups for an even chance; the quantum run made 25 oracle calls, and it did so after 1,060 gates. That gate count is the number to be careful with. The engine counts the oracle as one gate and the multi-controlled flip inside the diffusion as one gate, and real hardware pays many more for each of those, so 1,060 undercounts anything physical. The 25 is honest. The 1,060 is a floor.

LiveA square root, not an exponential3 min▶

At ten qubits the factor is 512 against 25, about 20. It is tempting to call that exponential, and the deck will. It is not. The number of oracle calls grows as the square root of N, so doubling the search space multiplies the quantum count by about 1.4 while it doubles the classical one. Read the widget's optimum column from two qubits to ten and the growth is plain: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 17, 25, roughly pi over 4 times the square root of N at every size.

Carry that to Brindle's scale. The 12-million-row customer table needs a 24-qubit index, since 2 to the 24th is 16,777,216 slots. Half of that is 8,388,608 classical lookups for an even chance. Pi over 4 times the square root of 16,777,216 is about 3,217 oracle calls. That is a factor of about 2,600, and it is arithmetic from the closed form, not a simulation, since the register in this page stops at ten qubits. A factor of 2,600 is real and is nothing like 2 to the 24th. Grover's abstract also says the method is within a small constant factor of the fastest possible quantum search, which means no better unstructured search is coming to change that shape. Square root is what there is.

Real world

In the constructed Brindle story the deck's headline said "exponential speedup on customer search". The analyst rewrote it on the whiteboard as "about 3,217 oracle calls against about 8.4 million lookups, assuming the oracle is free and the data is already loaded", and the room went quiet in a useful way. The second sentence is still a good number. It is just a different claim, with a different fine print, and session 3 is that fine print.

Self-studyThe oracle is the question, not the answer3 min▶

The oracle has been counted as one gate all night, and it is worth being precise about what it is. It is a predicate: a gate that, given a slot, flips its sign if and only if that slot satisfies the condition being searched for. It does not know where the answer is. It knows how to recognise one when applied to it. Somebody on the team would have to implement that predicate as a circuit, which means the condition "this customer matches the fraud pattern" has to be expressible as gates acting on an index, and the cost of that circuit is a real cost every honest count pays.

There is a second cost hiding under the first. The oracle reads the data it is testing, and that data has to be inside the register, which means it had to be written there, row by row, before the first oracle call. Tonight assumed that was free. Session 3 removes the assumption and prices the load, and practitioner session 5 counts the queries with the same engine. The headline from tonight survives both, but it arrives with a condition attached, and the condition is the point.

Part 3 · build-along

Check the two numbers, then rewrite the sentence

17 min hands on

No code. Six steps with the widget above and the two numbers you wrote down at the end of session 1. The aim is to leave with the mechanism in one sentence of your own, and a headline for the board that carries its own assumption.

Check your two numbers at ten qubits

Set the widget to ten qubits. Find the row marked the optimum. Beside your session 1 guess for classical lookups write 512, and beside your guess for oracle calls write 25. Note which guess was closer and by how much, and which of the two you were more confident about.

Stop at the top on three qubits, then go one further

Set three qubits. Read iteration 2, which is 0.945, and then iteration 3, which is 0.330. Say what happened in one sentence that uses the word rotation. If your sentence uses the word "overfilled" or "overflowed", it is the wrong picture; nothing spilled, the arrow kept turning.

Drop to two qubits and read certainty

Set two qubits and read iteration 1: 1.000000. This is the one size at which the search is exact, and it is why every quantum search demo starts here. Then read iteration 2 and note that certainty did not survive one more move.

Take ten qubits past the optimum

Back to ten qubits. The table runs to 28 iterations. Watch the probability fall from 0.9995 at iteration 25 across the three rows past it, and note the gate count still climbing. Every row past the optimum costs more and returns less, and no amount of hardware changes that direction.

Rewrite the deck's sentence

Take "evaluates all possibilities simultaneously" and rewrite it as a sentence about one vector, one sign flip, one reflection and one sample. It should be longer, duller, and checkable against the widget. Read it to the person next to you and ask them which number in the widget it predicts.

Write the two-number headline, and underline the assumption

For the board: "25 oracle calls against 512 lookups at ten qubits, under the assumption that the oracle is free and the data is already loaded." Underline the assumption. Session 3 removes it, and the headline will need to be rewritten, which is the reason to write it down tonight in a form that shows where it bends.

The habit to keep. When a claim says the machine does everything at once, ask for the count of oracle calls and the iteration at which the probability peaks. A mechanism has both numbers; a picture has neither. And when the count arrives, ask what it assumed about the data, because the next session shows that the assumption is where the headline is quietly paid for.

Check yourself

Three questions

A ten-qubit register has been put into a uniform superposition and nothing else. The deck calls this "all 1,024 possibilities at once". What does one read of it return?

A · All 1,024 answers, which the machine then filters
B · The correct slot, since it is in the superposition
C · One random slot, with a 0.000977 chance of being the target, which is exactly the odds of guessing without a register at all
A uniform register has equal squares everywhere, so a read is a uniform draw. The value of a search is added afterwards by the oracle's sign flip and the diffusion's reflection; the starting superposition on its own is worth nothing.

On three qubits the marked slot reads 0.945 after two iterations. Brindle's analyst runs a third iteration to be safe and reads 0.330. What happened?

A · Noise crept in, which is why real hardware is unreliable
B · The amplitude is rotating, and the third iteration turned it past the top; the optimum is about pi over 4 times the square root of N, and more iterations beyond it lower the odds
C · The oracle was applied to the wrong slot on the third pass
The simulator is exact and has no noise. Each iteration turns the marked amplitude by the same fixed angle, so past the peak it keeps turning and the probability falls, then rises again later. The optimum is a stopping point to be computed, not a limit the machine finds on its own.

At ten qubits the widget prints 25 oracle calls against 512 lookups. The deck calls this an exponential speedup. Which reading is honest?

A · The factor grows as the square root of N: about 20 at 1,024 items and about 2,600 at Brindle's 16.8 million slots, under the assumption that the oracle is free and the data is already loaded; it is real, bounded, and not exponential
B · The factor doubles with every added qubit, which is what exponential means
C · The factor is unlimited, since Grover's abstract says the method is the fastest possible
Doubling N doubles the classical count and multiplies the quantum one by about 1.4, so the ratio grows as the square root of N. Grover's abstract says the method is within a constant factor of the best possible quantum search, which bounds the gain rather than removing the bound. And the whole count assumes the data is already inside the machine, which session 3 prices.

Homework

Run every size and write the optimum beside the closed form

Two qubits to ten, one at a time. For each, write the optimum iteration the widget marks beside pi over 4 times the square root of N from the verdict line. Note that the optimum is the floor of that number, or one below, every time. That pairing is the square-root law seen nine times, and it is the fact to hold when a deck says exponential.

Bring a guess for the loading cost

Tonight assumed Brindle's 12 million rows were already inside the register. Write down a guess, in operations, for what it costs to put them there, one row at a time, before the first oracle call.
