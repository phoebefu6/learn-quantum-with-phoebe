Reading a speedup claim

Sessions 5, 6 and 7 each built one calculator: oracle calls against lookups, the load against the search, the error per gate against the depth. Today they become one instrument. A speedup claim is a number with its fine print removed, and the fine print comes back as five questions, each answered with a declared number or not answered at all. You build the checklist in numpy, run three constructed claims through it, and finish with one verdict line per claim that a board can read without a glossary.

Part 0

Three slides, one review

A speedup claim is a ratio: what the quantum method costs, over what something else costs. Both halves of the ratio hide choices. The top hides the load, the readout, the depth and the error rate. The bottom hides which classical method was chosen and how badly it was set up. A practitioner's job is not to believe or disbelieve the ratio; it is to put the hidden choices back, one at a time, and read what is left. Aaronson called this reading the fine print in 2015, and each of the last three sessions built one of the tools that reads it.

★ What you walk out with today. A five-gate checklist, each gate tied to a source and to a calculator you already own. A numpy scorer that takes a claim as a record of declared numbers and prints one status per gate: holds, conditional, fails or undeclared. Three constructed claims scored with it, ending in three verdict lines. And the one question that does more work than any other: against which classical algorithm, given the same access to the data?

LiveThe review the board asked for3 min▶

A claim reaches a data team as a slide, and a slide has room for one number. The number is usually honest about what it measures and silent about everything it leaves out. "Twenty times fewer oracle calls" can be exactly true and still describe a job that costs more than the classical one, because the oracle calls were never the expensive part. Reading a claim means asking, for each thing the number leaves out, what it costs, and writing the answer down with its source.

Real world

Brindle is a constructed retailer: 12 million customer rows, 4 million order rows, twenty years of encrypted backups, and an unnamed vendor's deck asking the board for a quantum pilot. After sessions 5 to 7 the engineer had three footnotes on three slides: a load term, a query table, a noise ladder. The board asked for something shorter. "Give us one line per claim: does it hold, and if not, where does it break?" Today builds the instrument that writes those lines, and runs it on three constructed claims of the kind such decks carry.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in code terms3 min▶
  • Claim. A record: what is computed, at what size, against which baseline, with which advertised ratio. Fields the slide does not fill are None, not zero.
  • Gate. One question the claim must answer with a declared number. Five of them, run in a fixed order.
  • Holds. The declared number survives the gate's calculator.
  • Conditional. It survives only inside a window the claim did not state, such as a range of searches per load.
  • Fails. The calculator, fed the claim's own numbers, removes the advertised advantage.
  • Undeclared. The claim gives no number for this gate. Not a pass, and not a fail either: a question to send back.
  • Baseline. The classical method the ratio divides by. Brute force is the easiest one to beat and the least honest one to quote.
  • Verdict line. One sentence per claim naming what survives and where it broke. The output a board reads.

Part 1 · covers the fine-print checklist

Five gates, each asking for one number

12 min live

The checklist is short on purpose: input loading, output readout, the classical baseline, noise and depth, problem size. Each gate comes from a source or a session, each has a calculator, and each asks the claim for one declared number. The figure runs one constructed claim through all five and draws what each gate charges for.

LiveThe five gates, and where each one comes from5 min▶

Aaronson's 2015 essay lists four caveats on the linear-algebra speedups, each of which can kill the advantage on its own. Two of them become gates directly. The other two, and his closing test, fold into the rest. Sessions 5 to 7 supply the arithmetic.

  • 1 · Input loading. His first caveat: the input must be loaded fast, and if preparing it already takes order-n steps the speedup vanishes "in the very first step". Session 6 priced it: one write per row, before the first query. Even the best proposed quantum RAM makes a query cost about log N; nothing in it makes the initial write of N classical values cost less than N writes.
  • 2 · Output readout. His fourth caveat: the output is a quantum state, and reading a specific entry costs about n repetitions. A search passes this gate, because it returns one index per run. A claim that returns a vector does not, unless it says how many runs it needs.
  • 3 · Classical baseline. His closing test: once the fine print is included, does a fast classical algorithm give the same information? Session 6 showed that an index built once beats the quantum search past 4,224 searches per load on Brindle's customer table. Part 2 takes this gate to depth.
  • 4 · Noise and depth. Not in Aaronson's list, which supposes a working machine. Session 7 and Preskill's framing: noise in the gates limits the size of circuit that runs reliably, so a claim needs an error per gate and a gate count.
  • 5 · Problem size. Session 5: the query advantage grows as sqrt N, so a claim is only as good as the size it was shown at. His second and third caveats live here for linear-system claims: the matrix must be sparse or structured, and well-conditioned.

Aaronson, S. (2015). Quantum machine learning algorithms: read the fine print. Nature Physics 11, 291-293. Giovannetti, V., Lloyd, S. and Maccone, L. (2008). Quantum random access memory. Physical Review Letters 100, 160501; arXiv 0708.1879.

LiveA gate charges; it does not veto4 min▶

The tempting way to use a checklist is as a filter: one failure and the claim is out. That is too blunt, and it loses information the board needs. A gate's job is to put a cost back into the ratio and report what is left. Claim B fails the loading gate for a single search, and the same calculator says it beats brute force from four searches per load. That is not a pass, but it is not nothing either: it is a condition, and a condition is something the vendor can be asked to meet.

So the scorer has four statuses, not two. Holds and fails are the obvious pair. Conditional means the advantage survives inside a window the claim did not state. Undeclared means the claim gave no number, and the right response is a question, not a verdict. A review whose rows all read "undeclared" has not found that the claim is wrong; it has found that the claim has not yet been made.

Real world

Brindle's analyst, reviewing the constructed deck, was asked by a board member whether "fails" on the loading gate meant the pilot was dead. She answered with the window: beats brute force from three searches per load on the customer table, loses to an index past 4,224, under the declared loading model with its three parameters printed beside it. "So the question for the vendor is how many searches we would run per load." That question went into the reply letter. The claim had not been rejected; it had been sent back with a number to fill in.

Self-studyWhy every number on the scorecard carries a label3 min▶

A checklist that quotes numbers without their assumptions is the same kind of slide it was built to read. Three labels travel with every number here. A gate count, such as 1,060 for ten qubits at 25 iterations, counts the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each, which undercounts hardware. A noisy success probability is the output of a declared one-parameter global depolarising channel, and that model flatters hardware. A loading total carries its three parameters: load 1 per row, query cost log2 N, and the number of searches per load.

The direction of each simplification matters more than its size. All three make the quantum side look better than it would on a machine. So when a claim fails a gate on this page's numbers, it would fail it at least as badly on hardware; when it passes, the pass is provisional. That asymmetry is what makes a modelled checklist useful: it can rule a claim out with confidence, and only rule it in with a caveat.

Part 2 · covers the classical baseline and what a credible claim declares

Against the best classical algorithm, not a scan

10 min live

The bottom of the ratio is where most of the damage is done. Brute force over a raw table is the classical method every claim wins against, because it touches every entry and uses no structure. The honest comparison is against the best classical algorithm given the same access to the data that the quantum method assumed. The worked case is dequantization: a quantum speedup that was believed exponential until a classical algorithm with the same data structure came within a polynomial of it.

LiveDequantization, the worked case3 min▶

Kerenidis and Prakash proposed a quantum recommendation algorithm in 2016 that samples from a low-rank approximation of an m by n preference matrix in time polynomial in the rank k and polylogarithmic in mn. Their paper says plainly what it assumes: the data sits in a classical data structure the algorithm has quantum access to, built from a plain array in linear time, and the speedup is exponential only if k is a small constant. Against brute force over the raw matrix, that looked like an exponential gap, and it was widely taken as one of the strongest candidates for one in quantum machine learning.

Tang's 2018 result gave the classical side the same thing: a data structure supporting l2-norm sampling, the classical analogue of the quantum access assumption. With it, a classical algorithm samples from the rank-k approximation in time polynomial in k and logarithmic in mn, only polynomially slower. Her abstract concludes that the quantum algorithm does not give an exponential speedup over classical. Nothing about the quantum algorithm was wrong. The comparison was. Leader session 4 tells the full story; how the recommender itself works belongs to learn-recommendation-with-phoebe and is not re-taught here.

Kerenidis, I. and Prakash, A. (2016). Quantum recommendation systems. arXiv 1603.08675; ITCS 2017. Tang, E. (2019). A quantum-inspired classical algorithm for recommendation systems. STOC 2019; arXiv 1807.04271. See also Leader session 4 · When the classical algorithm catches up.

LiveWhat a credible claim declares3 min▶

Turn the checklist round and it becomes a specification for the claim itself. A credible speedup claim declares five things, and each one fills a gate:

  • Problem size. The N it was shown at, and the N it is claimed for. If they differ, which numbers are measured and which are arithmetic.
  • Depth. The gate count of the circuit, and how the oracle was counted. A claim that counts the oracle as one gate should say so, as this page does.
  • Error rate. The error per gate assumed or measured, and the success probability it implies at that depth.
  • Wall-clock including loading. The cost from raw data to answer, with the load in it, and the number of searches or samples the load is shared across.
  • The classical baseline. Which classical algorithm, with what preprocessing, given what access to the data. "Brute force" is a baseline only if no structure is allowed on either side.
Real world

Brindle's reply to the constructed vendor deck was not a rejection. It was these five lines, sent back as a table with one empty column for each of the deck's three claims, and a sentence above it: "we will score whatever you fill in, with the arithmetic attached." A claim that comes back with the columns filled can be checked in an afternoon. A claim that comes back without them has answered the question too.

Self-studyWhy "when" is not a gate3 min▶

Claims often carry a date for when the hardware will be ready, and the checklist has no gate for it. That is deliberate. The three primary sources this track relies on take three different stances and none gives a year. Preskill 2018 treats noisy intermediate-scale devices as the near term and fault tolerance as arriving "eventually". The NIST IR 8547 initial public draft plans as if a cryptographically relevant machine may arrive within the secrecy life of data encrypted today, and says migration must begin even if such computers are a decade away; the 2030 and 2035 dates in it are proposed policy dates, not hardware predictions. Aaronson 2015 writes "supposing we had a quantum computer" and asks what it would be for.

None of the three resolves the others, and this page does not either. The five gates are questions a claim can answer today, with numbers. A date is a question no source can answer, so a claim that leads with one has put its weight on the one line that cannot be checked.

Preskill, J. (2018). Quantum computing in the NISQ era and beyond. Quantum 2, 79; arXiv 1801.00862. Moody, D., Perlner, R., Regenscheid, A., Robinson, A. and Cooper, D. (2024). Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards. NIST IR 8547 ipd, initial public draft, November 2024.

Part 3 · build-along

Score three constructed claims

20 min hands on

One file, six steps, run in order. Step 1 restates the three calculators from sessions 5, 6 and 7 in compact form; steps 2 to 5 build the scorer and run it on three constructed claims, none quoted from anyone. Nothing is sampled, so every output is exact, and the outputs below were printed by this exact code on Python 3.11 with numpy 1.26. Every number the claims print is one the canon already records.

Three calculators in one file

Session 5's curve and optimal k, session 6's loading tax and crossings, session 7's noisy success, plus the engine's gate count, which treats the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each and so undercounts hardware. Print the ten-qubit row first: the three numbers must be the canon's.

Pythonimport math import numpy as np def grover_curve(n, kmax, target=None): # session 5 N = 2 ** n target = N - 1 if target is None else target psi = np.full(N, 1 / np.sqrt(N), dtype=complex) curve = [abs(psi[target]) ** 2] for _ in range(kmax): psi[target] *= -1 # oracle: one call psi = 2 * psi.mean() - psi # diffusion curve.append(abs(psi[target]) ** 2) return np.array(curve) def best_k(n): # session 5 c = grover_curve(n, int(np.floor(np.pi / 4 * np.sqrt(2 ** n))) + 3) k = 1 + int(np.argmax(c[1:] >= c[1:].max() - 1e-12)) return k, float(c[k]) def loading_tax(N, R=1, load_per_row=1, query_cost=None): # session 6 log2N = math.log2(N) q = log2N if query_cost is None else query_cost k = max(1, math.floor(math.pi / 4 * math.sqrt(N) + 0.5)) per_search = k * q return dict(N=N, k=k, log2N=log2N, per_search=per_search, load=N * load_per_row, quantum=N * load_per_row + R * per_search, brute=R * N / 2, indexed=N * log2N + R * log2N) def crossings(N, load_per_row=1, query_cost=None): # session 6 r = loading_tax(N, 1, load_per_row, query_cost) gain, lag = N / 2 - r["per_search"], r["per_search"] - r["log2N"] lo = math.ceil(N * load_per_row / gain) if gain > 0 else None hi = max(0, math.floor((N * r["log2N"] - N * load_per_row) / lag)) if lag > 0 else None return lo, hi def gates(n, k): # the engine's count: oracle and flip as ONE gate each return n + k * (4 * n + 2) def noisy_success(p_ideal, p, G, N): # session 7 keep = (1 - p) ** G return keep * p_ideal + (1 - keep) / N k, ideal = best_k(10) print(f"step 1: n = 10, k = {k}, ideal {ideal:.6f}, gates {gates(10, k):,}") # step 1: n = 10, k = 25, ideal 0.999461, gates 1,060

The checklist as data

Five gate names in a fixed order and one function that prints a claim's scorecard and its verdict line. A claim is a dictionary from gate to a status and the evidence behind it; the status is one of holds, conditional, fails or undeclared, and the evidence is always a number from a calculator or the name of a source.

PythonCHECKLIST = ["input loading", "output readout", "classical baseline", "noise and depth", "problem size"] def run(name, results, line): print(name) for gate in CHECKLIST: status, evidence = results[gate] print(f" {gate:<19}{status:<12}{evidence}") print(" verdict:", line) print("step 2: five gates,", ", ".join(CHECKLIST)) # step 2: five gates, input loading, output readout, classical baseline, noise and depth, problem size

Claim A: twenty-four qubits over Brindle's customer table

Constructed: a search over Brindle's 12,000,000 constructed customer rows, advertised as about 2,600 times fewer lookups. The loading tax runs on the 12,000,000 rows; the advertised ratio is closed-form arithmetic on the 16,777,216 indices that 24 qubits provide, past the simulator, and the claim declares no error rate and no gate count.

PythonN = 12_000_000 t, (lo, hi) = loading_tax(N), crossings(N) pad = loading_tax(2 ** 24) # 24 qubits of index for 12,000,000 rows run("CLAIM A: 24 qubits over 12,000,000 customer rows, 'about 2,600 times fewer lookups'", { "input loading": ("fails", f"one search {t['quantum']:,.0f} against brute force {t['brute']:,.0f}"), "output readout": ("holds", "a search returns one row per run"), "classical baseline": ("conditional", f"beats brute force from {lo}, an index wins past {hi:,}"), "noise and depth": ("undeclared", "no error per gate, no gate count"), "problem size": ("undeclared", f"{pad['k']:,} calls against {pad['N'] // 2:,}: closed form, never run"), }, f"a window of {lo} to {hi:,} searches per load, and nothing said about noise") # CLAIM A: 24 qubits over 12,000,000 customer rows, 'about 2,600 times fewer lookups' # input loading fails one search 12,063,988 against brute force 6,000,000 # output readout holds a search returns one row per run # classical baseline conditional beats brute force from 3, an index wins past 4,224 # noise and depth undeclared no error per gate, no gate count # problem size undeclared 3,217 calls against 8,388,608: closed form, never run # verdict: a window of 3 to 4,224 searches per load, and nothing said about noise

Claim B: the ten-qubit demo

Constructed: one marked row among 1,000 demo rows, padded to a 1,024-slot register, found in 25 oracle calls against 500 lookups, at a declared 0.001 error per gate. This claim gives enough numbers to run every calculator, and the size gate counts the rises honestly: at 0.001 the trend falls, but not at every step.

PythonN = 1_000 t, (lo, hi) = loading_tax(N), crossings(N) k, ideal = best_k(10) # 1,000 rows fit a 1,024-slot register G = gates(10, k) claimed = noisy_success(ideal, 0.001, G, 1024) trend = [noisy_success(best_k(n)[1], 0.001, gates(n, best_k(n)[0]), 2 ** n) for n in range(2, 11)] rises = sum(b > a for a, b in zip(trend, trend[1:])) run("CLAIM B: 10 qubits, 1,000 product rows, '25 calls against 500 lookups, 20 times fewer'", { "input loading": ("fails", f"one search {t['quantum']:,.0f} against brute force {t['brute']:,.0f}"), "output readout": ("holds", f"one row per run, ideal success {ideal:.6f}"), "classical baseline": ("conditional", f"beats brute force from {lo}, an index wins past {hi}"), "noise and depth": ("fails", f"{G:,} gates at p = 0.001: {claimed:.6f} modelled"), "problem size": ("fails", f"at p = 0.001, {trend[0]:.4f} on 2 qubits, {trend[-1]:.4f} on 10, {rises} rise"), }, f"the 20 times is a p = 0 number; at its own error rate it succeeds {claimed:.6f}") # CLAIM B: 10 qubits, 1,000 product rows, '25 calls against 500 lookups, 20 times fewer' # input loading fails one search 1,249 against brute force 500 # output readout holds one row per run, ideal success 0.999461 # classical baseline conditional beats brute force from 4, an index wins past 37 # noise and depth fails 1,060 gates at p = 0.001: 0.346724 modelled # problem size fails at p = 0.001, 0.9910 on 2 qubits, 0.3467 on 10, 1 rise # verdict: the 20 times is a p = 0 number; at its own error rate it succeeds 0.346724

Claim C: exponentially faster recommendations

Constructed: a quantum recommender over Brindle's 4,000,000 constructed order rows. Only one gate here has an engine number, the load; the decisive gate is answered by a published result, and the scorer records the source as its evidence rather than inventing a figure.

Read the three verdict lines together

Put the three verdicts side by side, as the figure above does, and notice where each claim broke. Claim A narrows to a window and leaves two gates undeclared. Claim B gave every number and failed on its own numbers. Claim C failed on the baseline, where no calculator on this page could have caught it and one published paper did. Three claims, three different failure points: that spread is why the checklist has five gates and not one. Then open the scorer in Part 2 on claim B and set the error per gate to 0: the noise gate's evidence becomes 0.999461 and its status flips to holds, while loading and baseline do not move. One declared number changed one cell, which is exactly what a scorecard is for.

The habit to keep. Score before you summarise. Run every claim through the same five gates, write the status and the evidence for each, and only then write the verdict line. A summary written first picks the gate that agrees with it; a scorecard written first has to say where the claim broke, and a board can check the arithmetic behind every cell.

Check yourself

Three questions

A constructed claim says a ten-qubit search found one row among 1,000 in 25 oracle calls, against 500 lookups classically. Which gate does it fail first, and why?

A · Output readout, because a quantum computer cannot return a single row
B · Input loading: the 1,000 rows must be written in before the first call, so one search costs 1,249 against 500 under the declared model (load 1 per row, query cost log2 N, one search per load)
C · None: 25 against 500 is twenty times fewer, and that is what was measured
The 25 oracle calls are real, but they assume the data is already inside the machine. Writing 1,000 rows costs 1,000 before the search begins, and the search itself is 25 calls at log2 N each, 249 in all. The load alone is twice the brute-force bill, so for one search the claim loses; from four searches per load it starts to win.

A constructed recommender claim reports an exponential speedup over classical, measured against brute force on the raw preference matrix. What is the right response?

A · Accept it, because brute force over a large matrix really is that slow
B · Reject every quantum recommender, because Tang 2018 proved them useless
C · Ask which classical algorithm was compared, given the same data-structure access the quantum method assumed: Tang 2018 shows a classical algorithm with l2-norm sampling from that structure is only polynomially slower
The quantum algorithm needs its data in a preprocessed structure. Give the classical side the same structure and Tang's algorithm runs in poly(k) log(mn), so the exponential gap was a gap against the wrong baseline. Tang's result does not make the quantum algorithm wrong or useless; it removes the exponential claim.

A claim gives no error per gate and no gate count. How should the scorer record the noise-and-depth gate?

A · Undeclared, which is not a pass: on ten qubits and 1,060 gates the modelled success already falls from 0.999461 to 0.346724 at 0.001 per gate, so the missing numbers decide whether the claim works
B · Holds, because noise is an engineering detail the vendor will fix
C · Fails, because every noisy circuit returns the uniform floor
Without a depth and an error rate there is nothing to compute, so the honest status is undeclared and the honest action is to ask. It is not a fail: at 0.001 per gate ten qubits still succeed about a third of the time, modelled, and well above the floor. It is not a pass either: the same model at 0.01 per gate gives 0.001000 against a floor of 0.000977.

Score a fourth claim
