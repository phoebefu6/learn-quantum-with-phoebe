The fine-print bench

Nine sessions built the simulator, the gates, the search, the loading arithmetic and the noise model one piece at a time. This one puts all four benches in front of you with every input exposed. Move the qubit count, the iteration count, the searches per load, the per-gate error, and watch the numbers recompute on the real register in this page. Two things are built in on purpose: a break button that removes the oracle and leaves the search finding nothing, and a lever that makes the answer worse, which is adding qubits under realistic noise (every step at p = 0.005 and above). No machine, vendor or roadmap is named; the only hardware here is exact, and the page says where that flatters the result.

LiveWhat the four benches recompute on every press4 min▶

The first bench builds a uniform superposition over 2 to the n slots, marks one slot with a phase flip, and runs the diffusion step as five layers of gates, recording the target's squared amplitude after every iteration. It also counts gates, with the oracle and the multi-controlled flip counted as one each, which undercounts hardware. The second bench reads the optimum off that curve for every n from 2 to 10 and sets it beside the N over 2 lookups a classical search needs for an even chance, which is the figure Grover's own abstract uses.

The third bench is arithmetic on three declared parameters: a cost per row to load, a cost per oracle query, and a number of searches per load. It prints the quantum total, the brute-force classical total and the indexed classical total, and the two crossing points between them. The fourth bench applies a global depolarising channel to the ideal curve: after G gates a fraction (1 - p) to the G of the ideal survives and the rest is uniform. That is exact for the channel as declared and is a simplification of hardware, where noise is per qubit and structured.

LiveThe four runs worth doing first5 min▶
  • Leave everything alone. Ten qubits, 25 iterations, oracle on. Success 0.999461 at 1,060 gates, against a classical N over 2 of 512 lookups. The quantum run made 25 oracle calls. That is the only table in the sixteen sessions where quantum wins outright, and it assumes the oracle is free and the data is already in the register.
  • Drag the iterations to 28. Success falls to 0.9562 and keeps falling. The amplitude is a rotation and the optimum is a point on it, not a floor. "Run more iterations to be sure" is the wrong instinct and the bench shows it.
  • Drop to two qubits and one iteration. Success 1.000000. Four slots, one query, certainty. Then three qubits: iteration 2 is 0.945 and iteration 3 is 0.330, which is the figure above with the numbers live.
  • Switch the oracle off. At any n and any k the success reads exactly 1 over N: 0.000977 at ten qubits, 0.25 at two. The diffusion step maps the uniform state to itself, so with nothing flipped nothing climbs. The bench is measuring the oracle, not an artifact of the code.

Self-studyWhy the factor is sqrt N and why that is the whole story4 min▶

Each iteration rotates the state by a fixed angle, twice the angle whose sine is 1 over the square root of N, so reaching the target takes about pi over 4 times the square root of N iterations. At ten qubits that is 25.13, and the bench finds 25. At twenty-four qubits, enough to index Brindle's 12 million customers, it would be about 3,217 oracle calls against 8,388,608 lookups: a factor of about 2,600, and growing as the square root of N.

That is a quadratic advantage, and it is the honest headline for unstructured search. It is not an exponential one, and Grover's own abstract says the algorithm is within a constant factor of the best possible, so no better search over unstructured data is coming. Every larger claim about search is a claim about structure in the data, and structure is exactly what a classical index exploits for free, which is bench C.

Part 2 · benches C and D

The load and the noise

16 min hands on

Bench C is arithmetic on three parameters that every speedup claim leaves out and that you must declare. Bench D is the ideal curve with one error rate applied per gate, and it carries the one lever in these sessions that always makes the number worse.

Live Bench C, the loading tax. Three declared parameters, three totals, two crossing points. A model with its assumptions printed, not a measurement of any machine.

...

Modelled, every term. Quantum = load x N + R x k x query cost with k = round(pi/4 sqrt N). Brute force = R x N / 2. Index = N log2 N + R log2 N. Nothing here is a hardware figure.
LiveThree runs on the loading tax4 min▶
  • Brindle's table, one search, kindest load, one read per query. Quantum 12,063,988 against brute force 6,000,000. The load alone is twice the lookups it was supposed to beat. The index is 282 million and would be absurd for one search, which is the point: nobody builds an index for one search, and nobody should load a quantum memory for one either.
  • Take searches per load to 3, then 100. At 3 quantum edges past brute force. At 100 it is well ahead of brute force and still ahead of the index, which has not amortised. This is the window a claim lives in, and it is real.
  • Take searches to 4,224, then 10,000. The index catches quantum at 4,224 on this table and wins from there, because log2 N lookups per search is 24 and the quantum per-search cost is about 64,000. Now set the oracle to free and watch the window stretch, then set the load to 10 per row and watch it close. The deck prints none of these three parameters.

LiveFour runs on the noise ladder5 min▶
  • Ten qubits, p = 0.001. The ideal 0.999461 becomes 0.346724 at the optimum depth of 25 iterations and 1,060 gates. The best any depth can do under this noise is 0.3932, at 19 iterations: the optimum moved earlier, because a shorter circuit tosses fewer coins.
  • p = 0.005, then 0.01. 0.005895, then 0.001000, against a floor of 0.000977. At one error per hundred gates the ten-qubit search returns the uniform distribution to three decimals, and the best any depth can do is 0.0137 at four iterations, fourteen times the floor and nothing like an answer.
  • Set p = 0.01 and drop to two qubits. 0.9148. Twelve gates, so twelve coins, and most of the time none of them lands wrong. Now press the anti-lever nine times and read the column: 0.7257, 0.5643, 0.4114, 0.2082, 0.0903, 0.0191, 0.0033, 0.0010. Every press is worse. No press is ever better.
  • Set p = 0. Every number returns to the ideal and the anti-lever stops hurting, which is the honest statement of what the exact simulator in these pages is: hardware with p = 0, which does not exist.
Self-studyWhat the model leaves out, and in which direction4 min▶

A global depolarising channel says that when an error happens the whole register is scrambled to uniform. Real errors are smaller and more structured: one qubit flips, one phase drifts, and the register ends up in a state that is wrong in a specific way rather than uniformly wrong. That sounds kinder, and in one sense it is, since a single flipped qubit can sometimes be corrected. In the sense that matters for this bench it is harsher, because the model's oracle costs one gate and a hardware oracle over a loaded dataset costs many, so G here is a floor on the real depth, and every surviving fraction printed above is an overestimate.

The direction of the error is the point to carry. The bench's noise numbers are optimistic at every setting. The only way to get a larger success probability than it prints is to assume less noise per gate than the model, not more structure in it. That is the question to ask of any claim that a search of real size runs on real hardware: what p per gate, and what G, and the product of those two things is the whole answer.

Part 3 · the brief

What you write down for Brindle

11 min live

All four benches point at the same discipline: a speedup number has to arrive with the three parameters that bound it and the noise that erodes it. Ten minutes, on the Brindle pilot, in the form somebody else could check.

State the algorithm and its honest factor

Not "quantum search" but "unstructured search with a square-root advantage in oracle calls: 25 against 512 at ten qubits, about 3,217 against 8,388,608 at the 24 qubits our table needs". If the claim is not a named algorithm with a known factor, it is not yet a claim.

State the load, the query cost and the searches per load

"Loading our 12 million rows costs at least 12 million writes, twice the brute-force lookups; we would need at least 3 searches per load to beat brute force and would be beaten by an index past 4,224." Three numbers, declared, with the model named. Then say which of the three the vendor has not printed, which is usually all three.

State the noise and the depth

"At one error per thousand gates, the ten-qubit search returns the right answer 35 percent of the time; at one per hundred, it returns noise. Our table needs 24 qubits and a far deeper circuit, so the per-gate error the pilot assumes is the number that decides everything." Ask for it in writing.

Say what would change your mind

A per-gate error rate and a circuit depth, measured, for a search at the size you care about. A per-query cost, printed. A reason the data cannot be indexed classically. Write the triggers now, while nothing is on fire, because the version written during a budget meeting will protect the pilot rather than the reader.

LiveThe four numbers, on one card3 min▶

Search. 25 oracle calls against 512 lookups at ten qubits, success 0.9995, in a circuit of 1,060 gates with the oracle counted as one. A square-root factor, and Grover's abstract says no better unstructured search exists.

Load. 12,000,000 writes against 6,000,000 brute-force lookups for one search over Brindle's table. Quantum ahead from 3 searches per load; the classical index ahead again past 4,224. Three declared parameters, none of them printed in any deck.

Noise. 0.9995 becomes 0.3467 at one error per thousand gates and 0.0010 at one per hundred, where the floor is 0.000977. At p = 0.01 each added qubit makes it worse, every step, from 0.9148 at two qubits to 0.0010 at ten.

The oracle. Remove it and every success probability reads exactly 1 over N. The bench measures the thing it says it measures.

After the course

Homework

About an hour, on something real.

Find the three parameters in one real claim

Take any published speedup claim about search or machine learning on classical data. Find the load cost, the per-query cost and the searches per load, or record that each one is absent. The absences are the finding.

Re-derive one crossing point

With your own build from session 1 and the loading arithmetic from session 6, reproduce the 4,224 on Brindle's table. Then change the per-query cost to 10 times log2 N and report where the window goes.

Break your own bench

Remove the oracle flip in your build and confirm the search returns 1 over N. Then remove the X layer from the diffusion instead and confirm the closed-form check fails. A bench you have never broken is a bench you have never tested.

Check yourself

Three questions

At ten qubits the bench reports 25 oracle calls against 512 classical lookups. Brindle's analyst extends this to the 12-million-row table and reports "quantum search is 2,600 times faster". What is missing?

The arithmetic in A is right and the sentence is wrong. The square-root factor is real for oracle calls and vanishes for a single search once the load is counted; bench C prints 12,063,988 against 6,000,000. The window where it holds is 3 to 4,224 searches per load on this table, under declared costs.

Under p = 0.01 per gate, the ten-qubit bench reports 0.0010 at the ideal optimum of 25 iterations and 0.0137 as the best any depth can do, at 4 iterations. What does the second number tell you?

Both numbers come from the same curve with the same channel applied; they differ because fewer gates survive the channel better. The best-depth figure is the honest nuance and it does not rescue the result. Thirteen in a thousand is not a search.

With the oracle switched off, the bench reports exactly 1 over N at every qubit count and every iteration. Why does that matter for trusting the other numbers?

A bench that cannot report a worse number measures nothing. The break button produces the worst possible number, 1 over N, exactly, and the self test checks the same property on every load. The diffusion step is doing precisely what it should: mapping the uniform state to itself.
