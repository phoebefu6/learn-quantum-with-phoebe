Grover step by step

It turns out to be two moves on an array: negate one entry, then reflect every entry about the mean. Part 0

Brindle's engineer opens the algorithm under the word search

Every quantum search claim descends from one algorithm, Grover's, and the algorithm is small enough to read in full. It does not look anything up. It starts from a register where every index is equally likely, applies a sign flip and a reflection, and repeats that pair a number of times fixed by the size of the register. Leader session 2 argues about what the picture means; this session writes the code, counts the gates and finds the iteration where the answer is best and the one after it where the answer gets worse.

★ What you walk out with today. A numpy Grover of about forty lines that prints 0.1250, 0.7813, 0.9453, 0.3301 and 0.0122 for the target on three qubits, the same five numbers the page's engine prints. The gate sequence of the diffusion step, and the one line of algebra it equals. The rule for how many iterations to run, computed before running, and the reason one iteration too many is a worse answer rather than a safer one.

LiveThe slide, and the function it reduces to3 min▶

Strip the adjectives off a quantum search claim and it reduces to one function with three arguments: the number of qubits, the item being searched for, and the number of iterations. It returns a register whose target square is close to one, so a single measurement probably returns the target. The first two arguments are the problem. The third is the one no slide mentions, and it is the one this session is about: it has to be computed from the size of the register, and a larger value than the right one gives a worse answer.

Real world

Brindle is a constructed retailer, and its engineer is too. She started from the 12 million customer rows the deck gestures at. Twenty-four qubits give 16,777,216 indices, enough to hold them, and the closed form puts the optimum at about 3,217 iterations against 8,388,608 classical lookups for a one-half chance, a factor of about 2,600. Those three numbers are arithmetic from the closed form, not a simulation, because twenty-four qubits is past the simulator. She wrote them on the whiteboard, noted that session 6 still has to say what loading the rows costs, and went back to three qubits, where every line can be checked.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in code terms3 min▶
  • Oracle. The operation that marks the item being searched for. In the simulator, one line that negates the amplitude at the target index. On a real problem, a circuit that recognises the target, whatever index it is handed.
  • Phase flip. Multiplying one amplitude by -1. Its square, and so every measurement probability, is unchanged.
  • Diffusion. The second move of each iteration. As gates: H on every qubit, X on every qubit, one phase flip on the all-ones index, X back, H back.
  • Inversion about the mean. What the diffusion does to the array: each amplitude a becomes 2 x mean - a. Entries below the mean rise above it and entries above it fall.
  • Iteration. One oracle followed by one diffusion. 4n + 2 gates by the engine's count, which treats the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each.
  • Optimum. The iteration count k at which the target's square peaks. About pi/4 sqrt N; computed before running, never discovered by running more.
Part 1 · covers the oracle and diffusion

Two moves: flip one sign, then reflect about the mean

12 min live

Start from H on every qubit of three: eight amplitudes of 0.354. The oracle negates one of them. The diffusion replaces every amplitude a with 2 x mean - a. That pair is one iteration, and the figure draws it bar by bar on index 5, the same target the build-along uses.

LiveThe oracle is a phase flip on one index4 min▶

In code the oracle is one line: negate the amplitude at the target index. It changes no probability, because the square of -0.354 is the square of 0.354, so a measurement straight after the oracle sees exactly the uniform distribution it saw before. What it does change is the mean of the eight amplitudes, from 0.354 to 0.265, and the second move is built to exploit exactly that difference.

The oracle is the question, not the answer. On a real problem it is a circuit that checks "is this the item I want" for whichever index it is handed and flips the sign when the answer is yes. Building it needs a way to recognise the target, never its location. In the simulator the target is a plain parameter because the simulator is testing the mechanism; on any real search, whoever builds the oracle has to build the recogniser as a reversible circuit, and that cost appears in no count on this page.

Real world

Brindle's engineer (constructed, like everything about Brindle) wrote her first oracle as "set slot 5 to 1, everything else to 0". It found the item in zero iterations, every time. It was also not an oracle: it destroyed the other amplitudes, it could not be undone, and it needed the answer as input. The check that caught it is the one worth keeping: an oracle applied on its own must leave every square exactly where it was.

LiveDiffusion is five layers of gates5 min▶

As algebra the diffusion is 2|s><s| - I, where |s> is the uniform state; in numpy that is one line, 2 * psi.mean() - psi, the inversion about the mean. As gates it is H on every qubit, X on every qubit, one phase flip on the all-ones index, X back, H back. The H layers move the uniform state to the all-zeros state and back; the X layers turn "flip the all-zeros state" into "flip the all-ones state", which is the single multi-controlled flip a circuit can express directly.

Count it: n + n + 1 + n + n is 4n + 1 for the diffusion, plus 1 for the oracle, so 4n + 2 per iteration. That is 14 on three qubits, and with n Hadamards to start, 31 at the optimum of two iterations; on ten qubits it is 1,060 at the optimum of 25. Both numbers count the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as ONE gate each, which undercounts hardware: on a device both are built from many native gates, so these counts are a floor, never an estimate of real depth.

One wrinkle the build-along shows on purpose. The gate sequence produces I - 2|s><s|, the negative of the reflection, so every amplitude comes out with its sign flipped. A sign shared by every amplitude is a global phase and no measurement can see it. The page's engine builds the diffusion from gates, the one-line numpy version reflects, and the probabilities agree to the last digit.

Self-studyWhere it comes from: Grover 19963 min▶

Grover, L. K. (1996). A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search. STOC 1996; arXiv quant-ph/9605043.

The abstract's own setting is a phone directory of N entries in no particular order. Any classical method, deterministic or randomised, needs at least N/2 lookups to find the target with probability one half; the quantum algorithm needs on the order of sqrt N steps, and the abstract claims that is within a small constant factor of the fastest possible quantum algorithm. Its account of the mechanism is phases: the operations are tuned so the correct path reinforces and the others cancel. That is the sign flip and the reflection, described before anyone drew them as bars.

The abstract does not give the exact number of iterations. The pages derive it on the simulator and check it against a closed form, which is Part 2. Session 5 takes the N/2 against sqrt N comparison and counts it, query by query, from two qubits to ten.

LiveThree runs worth doing first4 min▶
  • Three qubits, four presses. 0.7813 at 17 gates, then 0.9453 at 31, against pi/4 sqrt 8 = 2.22. The third press gives 0.3301 and the fourth 0.0122, which is a tenth of where the search started. Every one of those numbers is the build-along's, to four places.
  • Two qubits, two presses. One press gives 1.0000 at 12 gates: certainty, the only size where Grover is exact. A second press gives 0.2500, straight back to the uniform start.
  • Five qubits, five presses. Four presses give 0.9992 at 93 gates, against pi/4 sqrt 32 = 4.44. The fifth gives 0.8596. The fall is gentler than on three qubits because each step turns the state by a smaller angle, but it is still a fall.
LiveCompute k before you run3 min▶

The rule from the canon run: the optimum sits at floor(pi/4 sqrt N) or one below it, at every size from two qubits to ten. On those nine sizes it lands on the floor each time, at 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 17 and 25 iterations. The reason for "or one below" is in the algebra: the exact best real-valued k is pi / (4 theta) - 1/2, where sin theta = 1/sqrt N, and because theta is slightly larger than 1/sqrt N that value always sits a little under pi/4 sqrt N, so rounding it lands on the floor or the integer below.

The widget scans the curve, and that is a luxury of the simulator. On a device you choose k once, run, and measure; there is no curve to look at and every measurement destroys the state. Computing k from N before running is not a shortcut; it is the only procedure that exists.

Self-studyPast the optimum the amplitude keeps rotating3 min▶

Each iteration is a rotation by a fixed angle 2 theta in a plane with two directions: the target, and the even mixture of everything else. The uniform start sits at angle theta from the "everything else" direction; after k iterations it sits at (2k + 1) theta, and the target's probability is the squared sine of that angle. Nothing in the two moves knows where the target direction is, so nothing stops the rotation when it gets there. On three qubits the second iteration lands nearest the target and the third carries the state well past it.

Real world

In Brindle's code review (constructed) a colleague suggested running "a couple of extra iterations to be safe", the way a data team adds retries to a flaky job. On three qubits that turns 0.9453 into 0.3301 and then 0.0122. A retry repeats an attempt; an iteration continues a rotation. The review note that went into the repository was one line: k is computed from N and is never a tuning knob.

Self-studyThe closed form, and the gap that vouches for the engine3 min▶

The probability after k iterations is sin^2((2k + 1) theta) with sin theta = 1/sqrt N. The engine checks itself against that formula on five qubits at every iteration on every page load, and the worst gap is 6.4e-15, which is floating point. The widget above prints the gap to the closed form on every press so you can watch the two agree, and step 5 of the build-along prints both columns side by side.

Two qubits is the case that makes the formula feel real. There sin theta = 1/2, so theta is exactly 30 degrees, and one iteration puts the state at 90 degrees: certainty. The canon curve on two qubits reads 0.25, 1.0, 0.25, 0.25, 1.0, a rotation in steps of 60 degrees that comes back to the top every third iteration.

Part 3 · build-along

Grover in numpy, from an empty file

20 min hands on

Open a fresh file or notebook and run each step in order; each one builds on the last. The statevector is one complex numpy array, both moves start as one line each, and step 4 rebuilds the diffusion from gates to prove the two agree. Three qubits, target 5 (binary 101), to match the bars in Part 1. Every output below was printed by this exact code on numpy 1.26.4. The probabilities are exact and will match yours; the counts in step 6 will not.

The uniform start

One complex array of length 8, every entry 1/sqrt 8. That is H on every qubit, written as its result. The target's square is 1/8 before anything happens.

Pythonimport numpy as np n, target = 3, 5 N = 2 ** n psi = np.full(N, 1 / np.sqrt(N), dtype=complex) def p_target(state): return abs(state[target]) ** 2 print("step 1: N =", N, "| every amplitude", round(psi[0].real, 4), "| p(target) =", f"{p_target(psi):.7f}") # step 1: N = 8 | every amplitude 0.3536 | p(target) = 0.1250000

The oracle: negate one entry

Copy, flip, return. The target's square does not move; the mean drops from 0.3536 to 0.2652. Check both before going on, because an oracle that changes a probability is not an oracle.

Pythondef oracle(state): out = state.copy() out[target] *= -1 return out flipped = oracle(psi) print("step 2:", np.round(flipped.real, 4)) print("step 2: p(target) =", f"{p_target(flipped):.7f}", "| mean amplitude =", round(flipped.real.mean(), 4)) # step 2: [ 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 0.3536 -0.3536 0.3536 0.3536] # step 2: p(target) = 0.1250000 | mean amplitude = 0.2652

Diffusion as the inversion about the mean

2|s><s| - I in one line: every entry becomes twice the mean minus itself. The target jumps to 0.8839, the others fall to 0.1768, and the norm is still 1.

Pythondef diffusion(state): return 2 * state.mean() - state one = diffusion(oracle(psi)) print("step 3:", np.round(one.real, 4)) print("step 3: p(target) =", f"{p_target(one):.7f}", "| norm =", round(float(np.sum(abs(one) ** 2)), 12)) # step 3: [0.1768 0.1768 0.1768 0.1768 0.1768 0.8839 0.1768 0.1768] # step 3: p(target) = 0.7812500 | norm = 1.0

The same diffusion, built from gates

H, X, flip the all-ones index, X, H, applied with the pair loop from session 1, counting every call. Fourteen gates per iteration with the oracle, which is 4n + 2 and undercounts hardware. The result is the reflection with every sign flipped: a gap of 1.77 to the one-liner and 3.3e-16 to its negative. A global sign, invisible to any measurement.

PythonH1 = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2) X1 = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]) gates = [0] def on_qubit(state, gate, q): out = state.copy() bit = 1 << q for i in range(N): if i & bit: continue j = i | bit out[i] = gate[0, 0] * state[i] + gate[0, 1] * state[j] out[j] = gate[1, 0] * state[i] + gate[1, 1] * state[j] gates[0] += 1 return out def flip_all_ones(state): out = state.copy() out[N - 1] *= -1 gates[0] += 1 return out def diffusion_gates(state): for q in range(n): state = on_qubit(state, H1, q) for q in range(n): state = on_qubit(state, X1, q) state = flip_all_ones(state) for q in range(n): state = on_qubit(state, X1, q) for q in range(n): state = on_qubit(state, H1, q) return state a = diffusion_gates(oracle(psi)); gates[0] += 1 # the oracle counts as one gate b = diffusion(oracle(psi)) print("step 4: gate-built p(target) =", f"{p_target(a):.7f}", "| gates in one iteration =", gates[0]) print("step 4: gap to 2|s><s| - I:", f"{np.max(abs(a - b)):.2f}", "| gap to its negative:", f"{np.max(abs(a + b)):.1e}") # step 4: gate-built p(target) = 0.7812500 | gates in one iteration = 14 # step 4: gap to 2|s><s| - I: 1.77 | gap to its negative: 3.3e-16

Compute k, then run k = 0 to 4

Compute the optimum first, then run past it on purpose and print the closed form beside each value. Seven places show the exact values: 0.78125 is a tie at four places, which Python's format rounds to even (0.7812) and the page's engine rounds up (0.7813). Rounded half up, the column is the canon's 0.1250, 0.7813, 0.9453, 0.3301, 0.0122, and the gate column is 3, 17, 31, 45, 59.

Pythonk_opt = int(np.floor(np.pi / 4 * np.sqrt(N))) print("step 5: pi/4 sqrt N =", round(np.pi / 4 * np.sqrt(N), 2), "-> compute k =", k_opt, "before running") theta = np.arcsin(1 / np.sqrt(N)) state = psi.copy() for k in range(5): closed = np.sin((2 * k + 1) * theta) ** 2 print(f"k = {k} p(target) = {p_target(state):.7f} closed form = {closed:.7f} gates = {n + k * (4 * n + 2)}") state = diffusion(oracle(state)) # step 5: pi/4 sqrt N = 2.22 -> compute k = 2 before running # k = 0 p(target) = 0.1250000 closed form = 0.1250000 gates = 3 # k = 1 p(target) = 0.7812500 closed form = 0.7812500 gates = 17 # k = 2 p(target) = 0.9453125 closed form = 0.9453125 gates = 31 # k = 3 p(target) = 0.3300781 closed form = 0.3300781 gates = 45 # k = 4 p(target) = 0.0122070 closed form = 0.0122070 gates = 59

Measure once, at the optimum

Stop at k = 2 and take 1,000 shots. The page's engine, drawing with its own mulberry32 generator on seed 7, puts 952 of 1,000 on index 5 (12 / 7 / 4 / 3 / 6 / 952 / 8 / 8). numpy's generator is a different stream, so your counts will differ from the engine's; the run below printed 945. Both are samples of the same exact 0.9453125.

Pythonstate = psi.copy() for _ in range(k_opt): state = diffusion(oracle(state)) p = abs(state) ** 2 rng = np.random.default_rng(7) counts = np.bincount(rng.choice(N, size=1000, p=p / p.sum()), minlength=N) print("step 6: exact p(target) at k =", k_opt, "is", f"{p[target]:.7f}") print("step 6: 1,000 shots:", counts, "| on target:", counts[target]) # step 6: exact p(target) at k = 2 is 0.9453125 # step 6: 1,000 shots: [ 6 9 4 9 14 945 7 6] | on target: 945

The habit to keep. Stop at the optimum, and compute k before you run. Write floor(pi/4 sqrt N) at the top of the file, run exactly that many iterations, measure once. A loop that runs "until it looks good" only works on a simulator, where you can look; on a device the look is the measurement and it ends the run.

Check yourself

Three questions

On three qubits after H on every qubit, the oracle for target 5 runs once. What has changed in the register?

The oracle is a phase flip on one index. A measurement right after it still returns the uniform 0.125 everywhere. What changed is the sign, and with it the mean, which is exactly what the diffusion's reflection turns into a taller target bar.

On three qubits the target reads 0.9453 after two iterations and 0.3301 after three. Why is the third iteration worse?

The simulator is exact, so noise cannot be the reason; session 7 adds noise on top. The probability is sin^2((2k + 1) theta), which peaks and then falls as k grows. The amplitude keeps rotating, which is why k is computed from N rather than raised to be safe.

The register grows from 4 qubits (N = 16) to 8 qubits (N = 256). How does the optimal number of iterations change?

The optimum is floor(pi/4 sqrt N) or one below: 3.14 gives 3 at N = 16 and 12.57 gives 12 at N = 256, both measured on the engine. N/2 is the classical figure for a one-half chance, and session 5 sets the two side by side.

Homework

About thirty minutes, with your build from Part 3.

Make n a parameter and run two to six qubits

Wrap the build in a function of n and target. For each n from 2 to 6, write floor(pi/4 sqrt N) down first, then run and record the k where the target peaks and its probability. Compare every row with the widget in Part 2; they must agree to four places.

Count the oracle calls

Add a counter inside oracle() and print it at the optimum for each n, beside N/2. That table, quantum oracle calls against classical lookups for a one-half chance, is where session 5 starts, and it runs it out to ten qubits.

Break it, after predicting

Write down what happens to the target's probability at every k if the oracle does nothing. Then make it a no-op and run. If your prediction was not "it stays at 1/N forever", the build or the prediction is wrong; the page's engine checks exactly this on every load.
