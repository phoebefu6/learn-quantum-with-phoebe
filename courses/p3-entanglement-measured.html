Part 0

"Entanglement-powered" is a claim about a joint table

Whatever entanglement does, a data team only ever sees it one way: as counts. You prepare two qubits, measure both, write down two bits, and repeat. After a thousand shots you have a four-row table of outcomes, and every honest statement about entanglement that a data engineer can check is a statement about that table. So the session treats it like any other dataset: the joint distribution, the two marginals, one summary statistic, and the question every analyst should ask before trusting a correlation, which is whether it survives a change in how you look.

★ What you walk out with today. The Bell pair and the product pair sampled on three seeds each, with the agreement rate computed for both. The reason neither qubit alone can tell them apart, drawn as two joint tables with identical margins. A classical mixture that fakes the Bell pair's counts exactly, and the two-basis test that exposes it, run live in the page and again in numpy. And one habit: before calling a correlation entanglement, change the basis and measure again.

LiveWhat the deck's phrase would have to mean in a table3 min▶

Two qubits, measured, give two bits per shot. If they are entangled, the two bits are linked in a way that no pair of independent qubits can produce. If they are not, the four outcomes 00, 01, 10 and 11 come out as the product of two separate coins. Either way, the only thing that leaves the machine is a column of two-bit strings, and the only thing anyone can do with it is count.

That gives the engineer a very plain test for any slide: which table would change, and how? If the answer is a sampled correlation between two bits, it can be measured, and tonight you measure it. If the answer is "faster analytics on customer rows", that is a speedup claim, and it lives or dies on the fine print that sessions 4 to 8 read. Entanglement is a property of the state. It is not, by itself, a speedup.

Real world

In the constructed Brindle story the engineer asked the deck's authors one question in writing: "What do your two-qubit measurement counts look like, and in which bases?" The reply was a screenshot of 00 and 11 only, about half each, captioned "perfect entanglement". Tonight is about why that screenshot, on its own, proves less than its caption says.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in data terms3 min▶
  • Product state. A two-qubit state that is qubit 1's column times qubit 0's column, as in session 2. H on each qubit gives one: 0.5 on every slot. The two qubits are independent coins.
  • Entangled state. A state that cannot be written as such a product. The Bell pair, H on qubit 0 then CNOT 0 to 1, is the standard example: 0.7071 on 00 and 11, zero on 01 and 10.
  • Joint distribution. The four probabilities of 00, 01, 10 and 11, the squares of the four amplitudes. Laid out as q1 rows by q0 columns, it is a 2 x 2 crosstab.
  • Marginal. The distribution of one qubit alone: the row sums or the column sums of that crosstab. P(q0 = 1) is the 01 slot plus the 11 slot.
  • Agreement rate. The fraction of shots in which both qubits read the same value, so (count of 00 + count of 11) / shots. The engine's QS.agreement computes exactly this.
  • Basis. The question the measurement asks. "As prepared" reads each qubit as 0 or 1. Applying H to both qubits just before measuring asks a different question of the same state, and some correlations survive the change while others do not.
Part 1 · covers the Bell pair against the product pair, sampled

Same marginals, different joint table

12 min live

Take the two pairs and lay each one's exact probabilities out as a 2 x 2 crosstab, qubit 1 down the side and qubit 0 across the top. The Bell pair's mass sits on the diagonal and nowhere else. The product pair's mass is spread evenly over all four cells. Now add up the rows and the columns: both tables give 0.5 and 0.5 for each qubit. Anyone who looks at one qubit at a time sees two identical fair coins. The difference is only in the cells.

LiveThe counts, three seeds each4 min▶

These are the frozen numbers from the page's engine, 1,000 shots per run, counts in slot order 00 / 01 / 10 / 11. The widget in Part 2 reprints all six on the spot.

  • Bell pair, seed 7. 460 / 0 / 0 / 540, agreement 1.000.
  • Bell pair, seed 11. 519 / 0 / 0 / 481, agreement 1.000.
  • Bell pair, seed 2026. 501 / 0 / 0 / 499, agreement 1.000.
  • Product pair, seed 7. 220 / 240 / 273 / 267, agreement 0.487.
  • Product pair, seed 11. 265 / 254 / 260 / 221, agreement 0.486.
  • Product pair, seed 2026. 249 / 252 / 250 / 249, agreement 0.498.

Three things to notice. The Bell pair never reads 01 or 10, not once in 3,000 shots: the squares there are exactly zero, so the sampler has nothing to land on. The split between 00 and 11 wanders from seed to seed (460 against 540 on seed 7) exactly as a fair coin would, because it is one. And the product pair's agreement sits near a half on every seed, which is what two independent fair coins give.

LiveThe agreement rate is a statistic you already compute4 min▶

Two columns, one row per shot, and the question "in what fraction of rows do they hold the same value?" is a match rate. Data teams compute it for duplicate detection, for label agreement between two annotators, and for checking that two systems wrote the same flag. Nothing about it is quantum. The engine's QS.agreement adds the 00 and 11 counts and divides by the total; the build-along writes the same line in numpy.

What makes it informative here is the baseline. Two independent fair coins agree half the time, so 0.487, 0.486 and 0.498 are what "no link at all" looks like over 1,000 shots. The Bell pair's 1.000 is as far from that baseline as a match rate can go. Keep the baseline in your head, because Part 2 shows a case where a match rate of 1.000 means something very different.

Real world

Brindle's engineer (constructed, like every Brindle detail) put the deck's screenshot next to a crosstab from her own order table: "paid online" against "shipped to home" across the order table, a match rate far above a half. Nobody in the room called that entanglement. It is a correlation in the data with an ordinary cause. The screenshot needed the same question her crosstab gets: what produced the agreement?

Self-studyWhy the marginals cannot help, written out3 min▶

P(q0 = 1) is the 01 slot plus the 11 slot. For the Bell pair that is 0 + 0.5 = 0.5. For the product pair it is 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. P(q1 = 1) is the 10 slot plus the 11 slot, and again both pairs give 0.5. So a pipeline that summarises each qubit separately, a mean per column, a histogram per column, a per-feature drift monitor, produces identical output for the two states. The sampled margins on seed 7 show the same thing with sampling noise on top: 0.540 for the Bell pair's q0 and 0.507 for the product pair's.

This is the same trap as two columns that each look fine and a join that does not. Any check that could ever detect entanglement has to read both bits from the same shot together. Per-column statistics are blind to it by construction.

Part 2 · covers the honest caveat: one basis is not proof

A classical box can fake the Bell counts; a basis change cannot be faked so easily

8 min live

Here is a machine with no quantum anything in it: on each run, flip one fair coin; if heads, prepare 00, if tails, prepare 11. Over a thousand runs it reads only 00 and 11, about half each, agreement 1.000. Its counts are the Bell pair's counts. The difference shows up only when you ask a different question of the same preparation: apply H to both qubits just before measuring. The Bell pair keeps its agreement. Each branch of the mixture spreads evenly across all four slots, and the agreement falls to about a half.

Live The page's engine on two qubits, 1,000 shots per run. Pick a state, a basis and a seed; every count, rate and probability below is computed on the spot. The mixture draws 500 shots from 00 and 500 from 11, the second half on the next seed up. This two-basis test is the simplest honest check, not a full entanglement witness; the formal tests, Bell inequalities, are named here and not taught.

LiveFour runs worth doing first4 min▶
  • Bell pair, as prepared, seed 7. 460 / 0 / 0 / 540, agreement 1.000. The canon run, reprinted from the engine.
  • Classical mixture, as prepared, seed 7. 500 / 0 / 0 / 500, agreement 1.000. Exact probabilities 0.5, 0, 0, 0.5: the same column as the Bell pair. On counts alone there is nothing to choose between them.
  • Classical mixture, H on both, seed 7. 235 / 241 / 277 / 247, agreement 0.482, every exact probability 0.25. Seeds 11 and 2026 give 0.461 and 0.483. The agreement was a property of the preparation, and a different question removes it.
  • Bell pair, H on both, seed 7. 460 / 0 / 0 / 540 again, probabilities 0.5, 0, 0, 0.5. H on both returns the Bell pair to itself, so the same seed draws the same shots. The agreement survived the second question.
Self-studyThe product pair's 1.000, and why agreement needs its margins3 min▶

Set the widget to the product pair and H on both. Every one of the 1,000 shots reads 00, so agreement is 1.000. That is not correlation. H undoes H, so each qubit goes back to a certain zero, and two certain zeros agree every time for the same reason two constant columns match on every row. The widget prints "q0 reads 1" as 0.000 beside it, which is the tell.

So the agreement rate is only informative when both qubits are uncertain on their own. The Bell pair is the one state of the three that agrees on every shot AND keeps each qubit at about a half, in both bases. Report agreement beside the margins, every time, or a constant will pass for a link.

Self-studyWhat the test is, and what it is not3 min▶

Two bases is the smallest experiment that separates the Bell pair from the coin-flip box, and that is all it claims. It is not a full entanglement witness. A classical device that knows in advance which two questions you will ask can be built to agree in both, and ruling that out takes measurements at several settings and a Bell inequality, a bound that any such pre-arranged classical model must respect and an entangled pair can break. Bell inequalities are named here and not taught; the simulator here only ever asks the two questions above.

For a data engineer the practical reading is narrower still. A vendor's screenshot of counts in one basis cannot establish entanglement, and even a two-basis result establishes only that the counts behave as an entangled pair's would on those two questions. Neither says anything about whether the analytics on the slide got faster. That is a separate claim with its own fine print, and session 8 reads it.

Real world

Back in the constructed Brindle thread, the engineer's follow-up was one line: "Please send the same counts with a Hadamard on both qubits before measurement." It costs the vendor one gate per qubit to answer. If the reply shows the 00 and 11 pattern again with each qubit near a half, the screenshot has passed the simplest honest check. If the reply never comes, that is data too.

Part 3 · build-along

Three states, two bases, one agreement rate, in numpy

22 min hands on

Open a fresh notebook or file in a scratch directory; nothing here writes files. The six steps run in one session, one after another. The exact probabilities are arithmetic and yours will match to the digit. The counts come from numpy's generator, which is a different stream from the page's, so your counts will differ from the widget's; the outputs below are pasted from one run of this exact code, and yours match them only if your numpy version draws the same stream. The figure is the pipeline every step builds a piece of.

Two 4-vectors and their exact probabilities

Write the Bell pair and the product pair straight down as amplitudes in slot order 00, 01, 10, 11, with qubit q as bit q of the index, the convention from sessions 1 and 2. Then compute the squares and the two margins. Both margins come out at a half for both states before a single shot is drawn.

Pythonimport numpy as np # slot order 00, 01, 10, 11 (q1 q0); qubit q is bit q of the index r = np.sqrt(0.5) bell = np.array([r, 0, 0, r]) product = np.array([0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5]) def probs(v): return np.abs(v) ** 2 def marginals(p): q0 = p[1] + p[3] # slots where bit 0 is 1 q1 = p[2] + p[3] # slots where bit 1 is 1 return round(float(q0), 4), round(float(q1), 4) for name, v in (("bell", bell), ("product", product)): p = probs(v) print(f"step 1 {name:8s} probs {np.round(p, 4)} P(q0=1), P(q1=1) = {marginals(p)}") # step 1 bell probs [0.5 0. 0. 0.5] P(q0=1), P(q1=1) = (0.5, 0.5) # step 1 product probs [0.25 0.25 0.25 0.25] P(q0=1), P(q1=1) = (0.5, 0.5)

1,000 shots and the agreement rate

A weighted draw over the four slots, a bincount, and the match rate. Your counts will differ from the widget's 460 / 0 / 0 / 540 and 220 / 240 / 273 / 267, because numpy's generator is not the page's. What will not differ: the Bell pair never lands on 01 or 10, and the product pair agrees about half the time.

Pythondef sample(v, shots, seed): rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) draws = rng.choice(4, size=shots, p=probs(v)) return np.bincount(draws, minlength=4) def agreement(counts): return (counts[0] + counts[3]) / counts.sum() for seed in (7, 11, 2026): b, p = sample(bell, 1000, seed), sample(product, 1000, seed) print(f"step 2 seed {seed:4d} bell {b} agree {agreement(b):.3f} product {p} agree {agreement(p):.3f}") # step 2 seed 7 bell [502 0 0 498] agree 1.000 product [257 245 245 253] agree 0.510 # step 2 seed 11 bell [520 0 0 480] agree 1.000 product [261 259 255 225] agree 0.486 # step 2 seed 2026 bell [464 0 0 536] agree 1.000 product [223 241 267 269] agree 0.492

The margins from the counts

The per-column view a monitoring pipeline would take: the rate at which each qubit reads 1. On this run the two states give the same pair of rates to three decimals, which is a coincidence of the draw, and the lesson does not depend on it: the margins are a half for both, so any per-column summary is blind to the difference.

Pythondef count_marginals(counts): n = counts.sum() return round((counts[1] + counts[3]) / n, 3), round((counts[2] + counts[3]) / n, 3) b, p = sample(bell, 1000, 7), sample(product, 1000, 7) print("step 3 bell q0=1, q1=1 rates", count_marginals(b)) print("step 3 product q0=1, q1=1 rates", count_marginals(p)) # step 3 bell q0=1, q1=1 rates (0.498, 0.498) # step 3 product q0=1, q1=1 rates (0.498, 0.498)

H on both, then measure again

H on both qubits is np.kron(H, H), and because both factors are the same gate the kron order from session 2 cannot bite here. Apply it to each state and repeat the measurement. Predict first: the Bell pair should come back unchanged, and the product pair should go to 00 with certainty.

PythonH = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2) HH = np.kron(H, H) # H on both; symmetric, so the kron order cannot bite here for name, v in (("bell", bell), ("product", product)): w = HH @ v c = sample(w, 1000, 7) print(f"step 4 {name:8s} after H on both: probs {np.round(probs(w), 4)} counts {c} agree {agreement(c):.3f} marginals {count_marginals(c)}") # step 4 bell after H on both: probs [0.5 0. 0. 0.5] counts [502 0 0 498] agree 1.000 marginals (0.498, 0.498) # step 4 product after H on both: probs [1. 0. 0. 0.] counts [1000 0 0 0] agree 1.000 marginals (0.0, 0.0)

The classical mixture

Two plain basis states, 500 shots each, added together, with the 11 half on the next seed up exactly as the widget does it. As prepared it reproduces the Bell counts. After H on both, each branch is 0.25 on every slot, and the agreement falls to about a half.

Pythonzero_zero = np.array([1.0, 0, 0, 0]) one_one = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1.0]) def mixture_counts(basis_change, seed): a, b = zero_zero, one_one if basis_change: a, b = HH @ a, HH @ b return sample(a, 500, seed) + sample(b, 500, seed + 1) for basis_change in (False, True): c = mixture_counts(basis_change, 7) label = "after H on both" if basis_change else "as prepared " print(f"step 5 mixture {label}: counts {c} agree {agreement(c):.3f}") print("step 5 exact, H on both, branch 00:", np.round(probs(HH @ zero_zero), 4), " branch 11:", np.round(probs(HH @ one_one), 4)) # step 5 mixture as prepared : counts [500 0 0 500] agree 1.000 # step 5 mixture after H on both: counts [234 262 258 246] agree 0.480 # step 5 exact, H on both, branch 00: [0.25 0.25 0.25 0.25] branch 11: [0.25 0.25 0.25 0.25]

The two-basis report

One line per state: agreement and the q0 margin, in both bases. This is the table the figure above draws, and the one to ask for whenever a slide shows correlated qubits. Only the Bell pair agrees in both bases while each qubit stays near a half.

Pythondef two_basis_report(name, counts_as_prepared, counts_h_both): rows = [] for label, c in (("as prepared", counts_as_prepared), ("H on both", counts_h_both)): rows.append(f"{label}: agree {agreement(c):.3f}, q0=1 {count_marginals(c)[0]:.3f}") print(f"step 6 {name:8s} | " + " | ".join(rows)) two_basis_report("bell", sample(bell, 1000, 7), sample(HH @ bell, 1000, 7)) two_basis_report("product", sample(product, 1000, 7), sample(HH @ product, 1000, 7)) two_basis_report("mixture", mixture_counts(False, 7), mixture_counts(True, 7)) # step 6 bell | as prepared: agree 1.000, q0=1 0.498 | H on both: agree 1.000, q0=1 0.498 # step 6 product | as prepared: agree 0.510, q0=1 0.498 | H on both: agree 1.000, q0=1 0.000 # step 6 mixture | as prepared: agree 1.000, q0=1 0.500 | H on both: agree 0.480, q0=1 0.508

The habit to keep. Before calling a correlation entanglement, change the basis and measure again, and read the agreement beside each qubit's own rate. One basis tells you the counts are correlated. Two bases start to tell you why. Neither tells you anything got faster.

Check yourself

Three questions

A monitoring job reports, for each qubit separately, the fraction of shots that read 1. Why can it never tell the Bell pair from the product pair?

P(q0 = 1) is the 01 slot plus the 11 slot: 0 + 0.5 for the Bell pair, 0.25 + 0.25 for the product pair. Both are 0.5 exactly, so more shots only sharpen the same answer. The difference lives in the joint table, so any check that could see it has to read both bits of each shot together.

A deck shows 1,000 shots of a two-qubit device: about 500 on 00, about 500 on 11, nothing else, captioned "perfect entanglement". Why is that not proof?

The widget's classical mixture prints 500 / 0 / 0 / 500 with agreement 1.000 on every seed, the same column of probabilities as the Bell pair. Counts in one basis are consistent with both. And a Bell pair's 01 and 10 squares are exactly zero, so sampling never puts a shot there.

You apply H to both qubits before measuring. What does that show for each of the three states?

H on both returns the Bell pair to itself and the product pair to 00, and turns 00 and 11 each into an even spread. The widget prints agreement 1.000, 1.000 and 0.482 on seed 7 respectively, and only the Bell pair keeps both agreement and uncertain margins. That pattern is the simplest honest check; Bell inequalities are the formal one.
