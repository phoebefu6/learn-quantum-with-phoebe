Counting queries

Brindle's board has asked its engineer for one chart: oracle calls, quantum against classical, as the search space grows. The chart is easy to draw and easy to get wrong, because the two bars count different things at different success levels under an assumption neither bar shows. Today you count the queries from two qubits to ten on the page's engine, rebuild the table in numpy, extend it to twenty-four qubits with the closed form, and write the three footnotes the chart cannot ship without.

Part 0

The board asks for one chart

Session 4 built Grover's search and found the iteration where it peaks. This session asks the question a board actually asks: how many times does each side have to look, and how does that number grow? The answer is a count of oracle calls, which is the unit the literature uses, and it is a fair unit only when you say out loud what one call costs and what it leaves out. The table is short, nine rows from 4 items to 1,024, and every row is measured on the register in this page.

★ What you walk out with today. A table you can defend row by row: oracle calls at the optimum for two to ten qubits, recomputed in numpy and equal to the page's engine, beside Grover's own classical figure of N/2. A one-line definition of a query. The reason the factor at 1,024 items is about 20 and not 1,024. A twenty-four-qubit row from the closed form, labelled as arithmetic. And the three footnotes every version of the chart needs: two success levels, a free oracle, and data that is already loaded.

LiveThe chart the board asked for3 min▶

The request is one sentence: show us oracle calls, quantum against classical, as the search space grows. It sounds like a plotting job. It is a definitions job, because "oracle call" means something different on each side of the chart, and the two sides reach different levels of certainty for their count. Before the first point is plotted, the y axis needs a label that a sceptical reader could check, and a footnote that says what the count does not include.

Real world

Brindle is a constructed retailer, and its engineer and board are constructed too. The board's question came straight from the unnamed vendor deck, which had one bar labelled "classical" and one labelled "quantum" and nothing on the y axis. The engineer's first move was not to open a plotting library. It was to write, at the top of an empty file, the sentence "one query is ..." and refuse to draw anything until she could finish it for both bars.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in counting terms3 min▶
  • Query complexity. The number of oracle calls an algorithm makes, as a function of the search space N. It counts calls and nothing else.
  • Oracle call. One use of the black box that answers "is this the target". Classically: read one row and compare. In Grover's loop: one phase flip on the whole register, followed by a diffusion that costs gates but no queries.
  • N/2. Grover's own classical figure: the lookups any classical method needs to find the target in an unsorted list of N with probability one half.
  • sqrt N. The order of oracle calls quantum search needs. At the optimum it is about pi/4 sqrt N, measured as 25 at N = 1,024.
  • Black-box model. The oracle's inside is hidden and treated as free; only the number of times it is consulted is counted. Every speedup claim about search is a claim inside this model.
  • Success probability. The chance that one run returns the target. N/2 classical lookups buy 0.5. The quantum optimum buys between 0.9453 and 1.0000 on the nine rows here.
Part 1 · covers what is being counted

A query is one look at the oracle, and nothing else

12 min live

On the classical side a query is a hand reaching into an unsorted pile, pulling one row and checking it. On the quantum side a query is one oracle call: a sign flip that touches the whole register, followed by a diffusion step that the query count does not see. The figure draws both, at 1,024 items, with what one query costs written on each arrow.

LiveN/2 is Grover's own classical figure4 min▶

The classical number on the chart is not a strawman picked to flatter the quantum side. It is the figure in the abstract of the paper the quantum side descends from. Grover's setting is a phone directory of N entries in no particular order, and the abstract states that any classical method, deterministic or randomised, needs at least N/2 lookups to find the target with probability one half. With no order and no index, there is nothing better to do than look, and every look rules out one row.

Grover, L. K. (1996). A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search. STOC 1996; arXiv quant-ph/9605043.

At 1,024 items that is 512 lookups. The phrase that matters is "with probability one half". Grover's classical figure is the cost of a coin-flip chance, and it is the honest figure to quote because it is the one the source quotes. What it does not give you is the cost of near certainty, which is the next card.

LiveThe quantum count, and the two success levels4 min▶

On the quantum side the count is k, the number of iterations at the optimum, because each iteration makes exactly one oracle call. From session 4 you know k is computed from N before running, and the canon run measures it at 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 17 and 25 for two to ten qubits. At ten qubits that is 25 oracle calls, and the target comes out with probability 0.9995.

So the two bars are not at the same success level. 512 classical lookups buy a 0.5 chance; 25 quantum calls buy 0.9995. A classical scan that wants near certainty has to be willing to look at almost every row, which is about N, 1,024 lookups. The page keeps Grover's N/2 because it is the figure the source states, and it says beside every comparison that the two sides are compared at different success levels. The mismatch here happens to work against the quantum side, which is a reason to state it, not a reason to hide it.

Real world

The engineer's first draft (Brindle is constructed) had two bars, 512 and 25, and the title "20x faster". The analyst from session 1 asked three questions in the review: faster at what success level, counted in what unit, and including what? The bars survived. The title became "oracle calls to find one item in an unsorted list that is already loaded", and two footnotes appeared under it.

Self-studyThe black-box model counts queries, not gates or seconds3 min▶

Query complexity treats the oracle as a sealed box: you may hand it an input and read its answer, and the only cost recorded is how many times you did. That is what makes the comparison clean, and it is also exactly what makes it incomplete. In the simulator an oracle call is one line that negates one amplitude; on a real problem it is a reversible circuit that recognises the target, and session 4 noted that its cost appears in no count on these pages.

The diffusion step costs gates and no queries. By the engine's count each iteration is 4n + 2 gates, so the ten-qubit optimum of 25 calls is 1,060 gates, and that number treats the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each, which undercounts hardware. None of it is time: a query count says nothing about how long a gate takes, how many shots a run needs, or how long it took to put the data where the oracle can see it.

Self-studyNo black-box search goes below about sqrt N3 min▶

Grover's abstract claims the algorithm is within a small constant factor of the fastest possible quantum algorithm for this problem. The result behind that claim is the Bennett-Bernstein-Brassard-Vazirani bound on black-box search, and Aaronson's essay names it as the bound Grover's algorithm sits at: any quantum algorithm that only learns about the target through oracle calls needs on the order of sqrt N of them.

Aaronson, S. (2015). Quantum machine learning algorithms: read the fine print. Nature Physics 11, 291-293.

This session names the bound and does not prove it. What it buys you is a sentence you can use in any review: for unstructured search the quantum gain is quadratic, and no cleverer quantum search will make it exponential, because the floor is already reached. Structure in the data changes the problem; it also usually hands the classical side an index, which is where session 6 picks up.

LiveThe factor grows as sqrt N4 min▶

Read the last column as you slide. The factor is N/2 divided by k, and since k is about pi/4 sqrt N, the factor is about (2 / pi) sqrt N: roughly 0.64 times 32 at 1,024 items, which is why the widget prints a number near 20 and not 1,024. Doubling N doubles the classical count and multiplies k by about 1.4, the square root of 2, so the factor also grows by about 1.4 per doubling. To make it ten times larger you need a search space a hundred times larger.

  • Slide to 2. Four items, 2 lookups against 1 call at success 1.0000. The only row where the quantum side is exact.
  • Slide to 3. Eight items, 4 against 2 at 0.9453, the lowest success in the table. Every row's success is printed because none of them is 1 by assumption.
  • Slide to 10. 512 against 25 at 0.9995. The widget prints the ratio to one decimal; the honest sentence is "about 20".
LiveThe twenty-four-qubit row, from the closed form3 min▶

Twenty-four qubits give 16,777,216 indices. The closed form puts pi/4 sqrt N at 3216.99, so about 3,217 oracle calls, against 8,388,608 classical lookups for a one-half chance: a factor of about 2,600. Those numbers are arithmetic from the closed form sin^2((2k + 1) theta) that the engine is checked against, not a simulation. A twenty-four-qubit register is two arrays of about 16.8 million doubles, past the ten-qubit simulator in this page, and the row is labelled that way wherever it appears.

Real world

In session 4 the engineer wrote this row on a whiteboard (Brindle is constructed, and so is its 12-million-row customer table). For the board's chart she drew it as a single hollow point to the right of the measured line, with the label "closed form, not run" beside it, and kept the measured nine rows solid. A reader who skips every footnote still sees which point was measured and which was computed.

Self-studyThe assumption under every row3 min▶

Every row in the table assumes two things it does not show. The oracle is free, so its circuit and its cost per call are outside the count. And the data is already inside the machine, in a form the oracle can consult. Kerenidis and Prakash state the second assumption plainly for their own algorithm and say it is the same memory model as any quantum query algorithm, Grover's included; they also say that building that structure from a plain array takes linear time.

Kerenidis, I. and Prakash, A. (2016). Quantum recommendation systems. arXiv 1603.08675; ITCS 2017.

Even the best proposed quantum memory makes a query cost about log N, and nothing in it makes writing N classical values cost less than N writes. Loading N rows is therefore order N, which is more than the N/2 lookups a classical scan needed for its one-half chance. Session 6 prices that load with a declared model and shows how many searches per load it takes before the table on this page means anything.

Part 3 · build-along

Recompute the table from an empty file

20 min hands on

One file, five steps, run in order; each step uses what the one before defined. The Grover function is session 4's, folded into a loop that records the target's probability after every iteration. Nothing here is sampled, so every number is exact and yours will match the outputs below, which were printed by this exact code on numpy 1.26.4. Step 4 asserts the table equals the canon; if it raises, the build is wrong, not the canon.

Grover as a curve

Session 4's two moves, folded into one function that returns the target's probability after every iteration. The target is the last index, N - 1, as in the page's tables. Print three qubits first: the five numbers must be session 4's.

Pythonimport numpy as np def grover_curve(n, kmax, target=None): N = 2 ** n target = N - 1 if target is None else target psi = np.full(N, 1 / np.sqrt(N), dtype=complex) curve = [abs(psi[target]) ** 2] for _ in range(kmax): psi[target] *= -1 # oracle: one call psi = 2 * psi.mean() - psi # diffusion: inversion about the mean curve.append(abs(psi[target]) ** 2) return np.array(curve) c = grover_curve(3, 4) print("step 1: n = 3 curve", [f"{p:.7f}" for p in c]) # step 1: n = 3 curve ['0.1250000', '0.7812500', '0.9453125', '0.3300781', '0.0122070']

Scan k and take the best, for n = 2 to 10

Scan one past floor(pi/4 sqrt N) by three, the same window the page's engine scans, and take the highest probability. A tie within 1e-12 goes to the smaller k, because fewer oracle calls is the whole point; on two qubits k = 1 and k = 4 both read 1.0000.

Pythondef best_k(n): N = 2 ** n k_cap = int(np.floor(np.pi / 4 * np.sqrt(N))) + 3 c = grover_curve(n, k_cap) top = c[1:].max() k = 1 + int(np.argmax(c[1:] >= top - 1e-12)) # ties go to the smaller k return k, c[k], k_cap rows = [] for n in range(2, 11): N = 2 ** n k, p, k_cap = best_k(n) rows.append((n, N, N // 2, k, p)) print(f"n = {n:2d} N = {N:5d} scanned k = 1..{k_cap:2d} best k = {k:2d} success = {p:.4f}") # n = 2 N = 4 scanned k = 1.. 4 best k = 1 success = 1.0000 # n = 3 N = 8 scanned k = 1.. 5 best k = 2 success = 0.9453 # n = 4 N = 16 scanned k = 1.. 6 best k = 3 success = 0.9613 # n = 5 N = 32 scanned k = 1.. 7 best k = 4 success = 0.9992 # n = 6 N = 64 scanned k = 1.. 9 best k = 6 success = 0.9966 # n = 7 N = 128 scanned k = 1..11 best k = 8 success = 0.9956 # n = 8 N = 256 scanned k = 1..15 best k = 12 success = 0.9999 # n = 9 N = 512 scanned k = 1..20 best k = 17 success = 0.9994 # n = 10 N = 1024 scanned k = 1..28 best k = 25 success = 0.9995

Set N/2 and the closed form beside each row

The comparison table: Grover's classical figure, the measured k, the closed form at that k, and the factor. The worst gap to sin^2((2k + 1) theta) is floating point, the same check the page's engine runs on itself at every load.

Measure the growth, then assert the canon

Divide each k by the one before. The small sizes jump about because k is a whole number; from six qubits on, the ratio settles near the square root of 2. Then assert both columns against the canon. If either assertion raises, stop and find the bug before quoting anything.

Pythonks = [r[3] for r in rows] growth = [ks[i + 1] / ks[i] for i in range(len(ks) - 1)] print("k(n+1) / k(n):", [round(g, 2) for g in growth]) print("mean growth per doubling, n = 6 to 10:", round(float(np.mean(growth[4:])), 2), "| sqrt 2 =", round(float(np.sqrt(2)), 2)) canon_k = [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 17, 25] canon_p = [1.0000, 0.9453, 0.9613, 0.9992, 0.9966, 0.9956, 0.9999, 0.9994, 0.9995] assert ks == canon_k assert [round(float(r[4]), 4) for r in rows] == canon_p print("matches canon: k and success, n = 2 to 10") # k(n+1) / k(n): [2.0, 1.5, 1.33, 1.5, 1.33, 1.5, 1.42, 1.47] # mean growth per doubling, n = 6 to 10: 1.43 | sqrt 2 = 1.41 # matches canon: k and success, n = 2 to 10

The twenty-four-qubit row, from the closed form only

No statevector here: two arrays of 16.8 million doubles would run, but the point is that the row does not need one and the board needs to know it was not built. pi/4 sqrt N, N/2 and their ratio, and a line in the output that says what kind of number this is.

Pythonn24 = 24 N24 = 2 ** n24 k24 = np.pi / 4 * np.sqrt(N24) half24 = N24 // 2 print(f"n = {n24} N = {N24:,} pi/4 sqrt N = {k24:.2f} -> about {round(k24):,} oracle calls") print(f"classical N/2 = {half24:,} factor about {round(half24 / round(k24), -2):,.0f}") print("arithmetic from the closed form; no statevector was built") # n = 24 N = 16,777,216 pi/4 sqrt N = 3216.99 -> about 3,217 oracle calls # classical N/2 = 8,388,608 factor about 2,600 # arithmetic from the closed form; no statevector was built

The habit to keep. Count queries, and say what a query costs, before you quote a factor. "512 against 25" is a true sentence only with its unit (oracle calls), its success levels (0.5 against 0.9995) and its exclusions (the load, the gates, the clock) attached. A factor quoted without them is a slide; with them it is a measurement.

Check yourself

Three questions

At 1,024 items the table shows 512 classical lookups against 25 oracle calls. Why is the factor about 20, and not 1,024?

A · Because the simulator stops at ten qubits; on a real machine the factor at 1,024 items would be the full 1,024
B · Because the gain is quadratic: classical needs N/2 lookups for a one-half chance and quantum needs about pi/4 sqrt N calls, so the factor is about (2 / pi) sqrt N, roughly 0.64 x 32
C · Because 25 iterations is a safety margin, and the true quantum count at 1,024 items is one call
The factor is N/2 over k, and k grows as sqrt N, so the factor grows as sqrt N too. At N = 1,024 that is about 20, measured. The simulator is exact, so a machine cannot do better on query count; and k is computed from N, never padded, because extra iterations make the answer worse.

A manager reads "25 oracle calls" as "25 steps of work". What does the query count leave out?

A · Nothing; in the black-box model a query is the whole cost of the search
B · Only the classical lookups, which are counted on the other bar
C · Loading the data where the oracle can see it, every gate in the diffusion and inside the oracle, and wall-clock time; it counts calls to the black box and nothing else
Query complexity counts oracle calls by design. The ten-qubit optimum is 1,060 gates by the engine's count, which treats the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each and so undercounts hardware, and none of that is time. Loading N rows costs order N, which session 6 prices.

A deck says quantum computers give "an exponential speedup on search". Why is that wrong for unstructured black-box search?

A · The gain is quadratic: k grows as sqrt N, so each doubling of N widens the gap by about 1.4, and the BBBV bound says no quantum black-box search needs fewer than about sqrt N queries
B · It is right, because the register holds 2 to the n amplitudes and the oracle touches all of them in one call
C · It is wrong only because today's machines are noisy; on an ideal machine the gain would be exponential
The table is measured on an ideal simulator and still shows a square root: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 17, 25 against 2 up to 512. Touching every amplitude is not the same as reading every answer, and the black-box bound Grover's algorithm sits at rules out doing better without structure.
