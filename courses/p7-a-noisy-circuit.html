Part 0

Every gate gets a price

Every number in sessions 4 to 6 came from an exact simulator: 0.999461 at ten qubits and 25 iterations, 25 oracle calls against 512 lookups. Exact means p = 0, a gate that never errs, and that is the one setting no hardware has. A circuit is a pipeline of gates, each of which can corrupt what it hands on, and the corruption compounds with length the way defect rates compound across the stages of a data pipeline. This session gives each gate an error rate, watches the success probability fall with depth, and finds that the one fix everybody reaches for first is the one that makes it fall faster.

★ What you walk out with today. A noise parameter in your own numpy simulator, one counter and one weight routed through every gate, that reproduces the page's ladder to six decimals. The ladder itself on ten qubits: 0.999461 at p = 0, 0.346724 at one error per thousand gates, 0.001000 at one per hundred against a floor of 0.000977, all modelled. The reason the best depth moves earlier under noise. And the anti-lever, measured: at p = 0.005 and at p = 0.01, adding a qubit lowers the success probability at every step from two qubits to ten.

LiveThe slide with perfect gates3 min▶

A search slide with a square-root curve is a statement about a circuit, and a circuit has a depth: the number of gates it runs, one after another, before anyone reads the answer. The slide never prints that depth and never prints the error rate per gate, and the product of those two numbers decides whether the curve means anything. Session 5 counted oracle calls. Today counts gates, gives each one a chance of going wrong, and reads what is left at the end.

Real world

Brindle is a constructed retailer and the vendor deck its board received is unnamed and constructed too. After session 6 the deck's search slide carried a loading footnote. The engineer added a second question beneath it for the review: how many gates deep is the search on our table, and what fraction of runs does the pilot expect to survive that many gates? The deck had no line for either. Today builds the arithmetic that line needs.

Self-studyThe vocabulary, in code terms3 min▶
  • Depth, G. The number of gate calls in a run. For Grover on n qubits at k iterations the engine counts G = n + k(4n + 2): 1,060 at ten qubits and 25 iterations. The oracle and the multi-controlled flip count as one gate each, which undercounts hardware.
  • Error rate, p. The probability that one gate call goes wrong. One number for every gate, declared on the widget.
  • Global depolarising channel. When a gate goes wrong, the whole register is replaced by the uniform mixture, every basis state equally likely. That is the model's whole picture of an error.
  • Keep. The weight of the ideal state still standing after G gates: (1 - p) to the G. One float, carried alongside the amplitudes.
  • The uniform floor, 1 / N. What a fully scrambled register returns for any one item: 0.000977 at ten qubits. A success probability at the floor is a guess, not an answer.
  • Ideal-optimal depth. The iteration count that maximises success at p = 0: 25 at ten qubits.
  • Best any depth. The iteration count that maximises success under the chosen noise. It is never later than the ideal optimum, and under noise it is earlier.
  • Modelled. Every noisy number on this page is the output of the declared one-parameter channel, never a measurement of any machine, and the model flatters hardware.
Self-studyWhy a data team already knows this shape2 min▶

A pipeline of twenty stages, each of which drops one record in a hundred, delivers about 82 percent of its input. Nobody is surprised, because everyone has watched a long pipeline leak. The quantum version has the same compounding and one extra twist: a stage that goes wrong does not drop a record, it scrambles the whole register, and a scrambled register does not look broken. It returns an answer, just a uniformly random one, with the same confidence as a correct run. There is no null to filter and no row count to check. The only defence is the arithmetic before the run, which is what the rest of this session builds. (The pipeline figures in this card are a constructed illustration.)

Part 1 · covers the depolarising channel, one gate at a time

One gate, one coin

12 min live

The model is one sentence: each gate does its job with probability 1 - p, and with probability p it replaces the register with the uniform mixture. Draw that for one gate and the rest of the session is multiplication. The figure follows the probability of the item being searched for through a single gate and shows where the leaked share goes.

LiveWhy one float carries the whole channel4 min▶

A register that is right with weight w and scrambled with weight 1 - w is not a statevector any more; it is a mixture, and the full description is a density matrix, N by N, which at ten qubits is a million entries. The channel never needs it. The scrambled part is the uniform mixture, the identity divided by N, and every gate in the set leaves the identity exactly where it is, because a gate only rotates the register and the uniform mixture looks the same from every angle. So after any number of gates the state is keep times the ideal state plus (1 - keep) times uniform, and only keep changes.

That is why the engine can run the exact statevector for the ideal part and carry one extra number for the noise. Each gate multiplies keep by (1 - p). At the end, the probability of reading the target is the target's diagonal entry of that mixture: keep times the ideal square, plus (1 - keep) over N. It is one line in the engine, noisySuccess, and one line in your build.

LivePredict before you run: (0.99) to the 1,0604 min▶

Write the expected number first, as in session 1. For small p, (1 - p) to the G is close to e to the minus pG, so the only thing to compute in your head is the product of the error rate and the depth. At p = 0.01 and G = 1,060 that product is about 10.6, and e to the minus 10.6 is about two in a hundred thousand: keep is 0.00002. Almost nothing of the ideal survives, so the success probability is the floor plus a crumb, 0.001000 against 0.000977.

At p = 0.001 the product is about 1.06, and e to the minus 1.06 is a little over a third. The engine prints 0.346724: about a third of the ideal 0.999461, plus a sliver of floor. If your prediction and the engine disagree by more than rounding, one of them is wrong, and the order of trust is the one session 1 set: the prediction, written before the run, is the thing you check the code against.

Real world

Brindle's analyst, asked by the board what "a small error rate" would cost, did not open a notebook. She wrote pG on the whiteboard: the per-gate error the pilot assumes, times the depth of the search. Below one, she said, most runs survive. Around one, about a third do. Past ten, the machine is returning random rows with confidence. The figures were from the page's modelled ladder, and she said so; the board's question became "what is pG for us", which is the right question.

Self-studyWhat the model leaves out, and in which direction4 min▶

Three simplifications, all declared. First, real errors are local: one qubit flips, one phase drifts, two neighbours couple when they should not. A global channel replaces all of that with one uniform scramble. Second, the engine counts the oracle and the multi-controlled flip as one gate each; a hardware oracle over loaded data and a ten-qubit controlled flip each decompose into many native gates, so the real G is far larger than 1,060. Third, nothing is sampled here: the success probability is the exact average over every way the coins could land, not one noisy run.

The direction is what matters. The second simplification alone makes every noisy number on this page an overestimate, because a larger G can only lower keep. Structured noise is sometimes kinder to a specific answer and sometimes harsher, which is why error correction exists and why it is not taught here. The page's position is the honest one: the model is a floor on the damage, and a claim that beats it should say which of the three simplifications it relies on.

  • Move to p = 0.01. The modelled column at iteration 25 reads 0.001000 against a floor of 0.000977. The best any depth can do is 0.013693 at four iterations: fourteen times the floor and nothing like an answer. Run it a thousand times and the target turns up about as often as any other row.
  • Read the second table at p = 0.005. There is no row where adding a qubit helps. Now go back to p = 0.001 and find the one row that rises, between four and five qubits. The noise did not get kinder there: the exact optimum itself jumps from 0.9613 to 0.9992, and at one error per thousand gates that jump outweighs the 35 extra gates. The trend still falls to ten qubits, and the widget says so rather than hiding the wobble.
LiveThe anti-lever: add more qubits3 min▶

When a search is too small, the reflex is to make the register bigger. At the error rates the canon measured, 0.005 and 0.01 per gate, this lever turns one way only; at gentler rates the trend is the same with the odd wobble where the exact optimum jumps. Each added qubit doubles the number of items, so the ideal iteration count grows as the square root of N, and each iteration also gets longer, 4n + 2 gates. Depth grows on both counts and keep falls with it. At p = 0.01 the anti-lever table reads 0.9148 at two qubits, 0.5643 at four, 0.2082 at six, 0.0191 at eight and 0.0010 at ten, modelled. Every number is worse than the one before it.

This is the framing Preskill gave the field in 2018. Noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices, NISQ in his acronym, are limited by noise in their gates, which limits the size of circuit that can be run reliably; the longer path runs through more accurate gates and, "eventually", fault tolerance, for which his abstract gives no date. The ladder above is that sentence measured on a model: the size of circuit that runs reliably is set by pG, and adding qubits raises G.

Preskill, J. (2018). Quantum computing in the NISQ era and beyond. Quantum 2, 79; arXiv 1801.00862.

Real world

The constructed vendor deck's answer to a too-small demo was the word "scale". Brindle's 12,000,000 constructed customer rows need 24 qubits of index, and the closed form the engine is checked against puts the ideal search at about 3,217 iterations against 25 here, each iteration longer than a ten-qubit one. That is arithmetic past the simulator, not a simulation, and the engineer wrote it beside the anti-lever table: the deck's lever is the one that, at every error rate above zero, makes the number worse.

Self-studyWhat the ladder does not tell you: when3 min▶

The ladder says what an error rate does to a circuit of a given depth. It says nothing about when hardware will reach any particular error rate, and neither does any source this track relies on. Three primary sources take three different stances, and none gives a year. Preskill 2018 treats noisy devices as the near term and fault tolerance as arriving "eventually". The NIST IR 8547 initial public draft plans as if a cryptographically relevant machine may arrive within the secrecy life of data encrypted today, and says migration must start even if such computers are a decade away. Aaronson 2015 writes "supposing we had a quantum computer" and asks what it would be for.

None of the three resolves the others, and this page does not either. The useful move for a data team is to stop asking when and start asking what pG a claim assumes, which is a number a vendor can be asked to write down. Session 5 of the leader track takes the timeline to depth.

Moody, D., Perlner, R., Regenscheid, A., Robinson, A. and Cooper, D. (2024). Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards. NIST IR 8547 ipd, initial public draft, November 2024. Aaronson, S. (2015). Quantum machine learning algorithms: read the fine print. Nature Physics 11, 291-293.

Part 3 · build-along

Add a noise parameter to your simulator

20 min hands on

One file, six steps, run in order; each step uses what the one before defined. Step 1 restates the pieces you built in sessions 1 to 4 with two additions: a gate counter and a keep weight that every gate multiplies by (1 - p). Nothing is sampled, so every number is exact, and the outputs below were printed by this exact code on Python 3.11 with numpy 1.26. Step 4 asserts the ladder against the canon; if it raises, the build is wrong, not the canon.

A gate counter with a keep weight

Your simulator from sessions 1 to 4, with every gate routed through one function, tick, that counts the gate and multiplies keep by (1 - p). That is the whole noise model. The success function reads the target's entry of the mixture. Run it at p = 0 first: it must reproduce the exact canon.

Pythonimport math import numpy as np def new_state(n, p_noise=0.0): N = 1 << n re = np.zeros(N); im = np.zeros(N); re[0] = 1.0 return {"n": n, "N": N, "re": re, "im": im, "gates": 0, "p": p_noise, "keep": 1.0} def tick(s): # every gate call goes through here s["gates"] += 1 s["keep"] *= (1.0 - s["p"]) # the channel: a share p of the register leaks to uniform def apply_h(s, q): bit = 1 << q i = np.arange(s["N"]); i = i[(i & bit) == 0]; j = i | bit ar, ai = s["re"][i].copy(), s["im"][i].copy() br, bi = s["re"][j].copy(), s["im"][j].copy() r = np.sqrt(0.5) s["re"][i] = (ar + br) * r; s["im"][i] = (ai + bi) * r s["re"][j] = (ar - br) * r; s["im"][j] = (ai - bi) * r tick(s) def apply_x(s, q): bit = 1 << q i = np.arange(s["N"]); i = i[(i & bit) == 0]; j = i | bit for k in ("re", "im"): a = s[k][i].copy(); s[k][i] = s[k][j]; s[k][j] = a tick(s) def phase_flip(s, idx): s["re"][idx] = -s["re"][idx]; s["im"][idx] = -s["im"][idx] tick(s) def diffusion(s): for q in range(s["n"]): apply_h(s, q) for q in range(s["n"]): apply_x(s, q) phase_flip(s, s["N"] - 1) for q in range(s["n"]): apply_x(s, q) for q in range(s["n"]): apply_h(s, q) def success(s, target): # target entry of keep * ideal + (1 - keep) * uniform ideal = s["re"][target] ** 2 + s["im"][target] ** 2 return s["keep"] * ideal + (1.0 - s["keep"]) / s["N"] def noisy_curve(n, target, kmax, p_noise): s = new_state(n, p_noise) for q in range(n): apply_h(s, q) out = [(0, s["gates"], success(s, target))] for k in range(1, kmax + 1): phase_flip(s, target); diffusion(s) out.append((k, s["gates"], success(s, target))) return out c = noisy_curve(10, 0, 25, 0.0) print("step 1: k = %d, gates = %d, success = %.6f" % c[-1]) # step 1: k = 25, gates = 1060, success = 0.999461

Pythonprint("step 2: predicted keep = 0.99 ** 1060 = %.5f" % (0.99 ** 1060)) c = noisy_curve(10, 0, 25, 0.01) print(" k = %d, gates = %d, success = %.6f, floor 1/N = %.6f" % (c[-1] + (1 / 1024,))) # step 2: predicted keep = 0.99 ** 1060 = 0.00002 # k = 25, gates = 1060, success = 0.001000, floor 1/N = 0.000977

The ladder: five error rates, two columns each

For each p, run the curve to 28 iterations, three past the ideal optimum, and read two numbers: success at iteration 25, and the best success at any depth with the iteration that gives it. The second column is the one the exact simulator could never show you.

Pythonkmax = math.floor(math.pi / 4 * math.sqrt(1024)) + 3 ladder = {} print("step 3: p at k=25 best any depth at k") for p in (0, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02): curve = noisy_curve(10, 0, kmax, p) at25 = curve[25][2] bk, _, bp = max(curve, key=lambda r: r[2]) ladder[p] = (at25, bp, bk) print(" %-6g %.6f %.6f %d" % (p, at25, bp, bk)) # step 3: p at k=25 best any depth at k # 0 0.999461 0.999461 25 # 0.001 0.346724 0.393189 19 # 0.005 0.005895 0.046121 8 # 0.01 0.001000 0.013693 4 # 0.02 0.000977 0.004457 2

Assert the canon

Fifteen numbers, every rung of the ladder, checked to six decimals. If this raises, the error is in your build: most often a gate that skips tick, which leaves keep too high and the noisy numbers too kind.

Pythoncanon = {0: (0.999461, 0.999461, 25), 0.001: (0.346724, 0.393189, 19), 0.005: (0.005895, 0.046121, 8), 0.01: (0.001000, 0.013693, 4), 0.02: (0.000977, 0.004457, 2)} for p, (a, b, k) in canon.items(): assert round(ladder[p][0], 6) == a and round(ladder[p][1], 6) == b and ladder[p][2] == k, p print("step 4: all five rungs match the canon to six decimals") # step 4: all five rungs match the canon to six decimals

Pull the anti-lever

For n from 2 to 10, find the ideal-optimal iteration count, then read success at that depth under p = 0.005 and p = 0.01. This is the table the figure above draws. Read down either noisy column and look for a row that goes up.

Break it on purpose

Move the keep update out of tick and into apply_h only, so X gates and phase flips stop counting as risk while the gate counter still says 1,060. Rerun from step 1. Step 4 raises AssertionError: 0.001 on the first noisy rung, because a build that forgets some gates reports a success probability that is too kind. That is the fault to look for first in any noisy number you are handed: which gates were left out of G.

The habit to keep. Before any run, write pG: the error per gate times the depth. Below one, most runs survive; around one, a third or so; past ten, the circuit returns the floor with confidence. A claim that does not print both numbers has not told you whether it works, and a claim that prints a G has still to say whether its oracle counted as one gate.

Check yourself

Three questions

On ten qubits at p = 0.01 the modelled success at 25 iterations is 0.001000. What does that number tell you?

With 1,060 gates at one error per hundred, keep is 0.00002 and almost nothing of the ideal state survives. 0.001000 is the floor plus a crumb. A thousand runs would return the target about as often as each of the other 1,023 items, so the most frequent result is decided by the random draw, not the oracle.

At p = 0.001 the ideal optimum is 25 iterations, but the best modelled success, 0.393189, comes at 19. Why does noise move the optimum earlier?

The ideal curve flattens as it approaches its peak, so the last few iterations add very little amplitude. Each still costs 4n + 2 = 42 gates, and each gate multiplies keep by 0.999. The product of the two peaks earlier. The amplitudes themselves are untouched: the same exact statevector, weighted by a smaller keep.

A constructed pilot proposal says the ten-qubit demo is too small and the fix is a bigger register at the same error rate per gate. What does the anti-lever table predict?

The table falls at every row at p = 0.005 and at p = 0.01: at p = 0.005 from 0.9562 at two qubits to 0.0059 at ten, modelled. A fixed per-gate error is not a fixed per-run error; the per-run survival is (1 - p) to the G, and G is what a bigger register raises.
